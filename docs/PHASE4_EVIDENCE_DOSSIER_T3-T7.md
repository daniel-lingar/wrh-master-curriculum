### 1.3 What the scripts do and do not specify (from the source text)

Recorded because the evidence comparison turns on it:

- The counted pattern is inhale 4 counts, an unspecified hold ("hold…"), exhale 6 counts. The script does **not** state the hold duration, the number of repetitions, breath depth, or a total duration. If counts are read as seconds, one cycle is at least 10 seconds plus the hold — i.e., at or below roughly 6 breaths per minute — but the script itself does not say this.
- ER-278's form is imperative: "Do it. Again. Good." ER-023 and ER-332 pair the breathing/grounding instruction with traffic-light opt-out language ("Yellow light. Red light. You run this room."), which on its face offers stepping out or leaving; the breathing instruction itself is phrased as a direction, not an invitation, in the variants inspected.
- ER-249/ER-257 label the feet-pressure/straw-exhale maneuver "your reset," and ER-023's Anchor purpose is "Re-establish safety and control." The implied effect is regulatory: the instruction is presented as the thing to do when a participant's system "lights up" or "starts to get loud."
- The scripts inspected contain no screening step (e.g., respiratory conditions, panic history), no instruction for what a participant should do if the breathing itself feels worse, and no facilitator escalation step attached to the Anchor itself. (Escalation material exists elsewhere in the curriculum and is backlog theme T2 / the Crisis Package; it is outside this family's scope.)

### T3-S02 — Balban et al. 2023 (cyclic sighing / structured respiration RCT)

- **Citation:** Balban, M. Y., Neri, E., Kogon, M. M., Weed, L., Nouriani, B., Jo, B., Holl, G., Zeitzer, J. M., Spiegel, D., & Huberman, A. D. (2023). Brief structured respiration practices enhance mood and reduce physiological arousal. *Cell Reports Medicine*, 4(1), 100895. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895 · PMID: 36630953 · PMCID: PMC9873947 · Trial registration: NCT05304000 (registered retrospectively, per the paper).
- **Inspection depth:** Full text inspected (summary, introduction, results, discussion, limitations, and STAR Methods including participant eligibility and protocol descriptions), via the PubMed Central full text.
- **Design / population / setting:** Remote, randomized, controlled study. 108 participants completed enrollment into four arms — cyclic sighing (n=30), box breathing (n=21), cyclic hyperventilation with retention (n=33), mindfulness meditation (n=24) — practicing 5 minutes daily for 28 days at a time of their choosing, with daily pre/post self-report (PANAS, STAI state anxiety) and continuous wearable data (WHOOP: resting heart rate, respiratory rate, HRV-RMSSD, sleep). Recruitment was mostly from a Stanford University undergraduate psychology class, with some word of mouth. **Exclusions, stated in the paper "for health and safety reasons": self-reported moderate to severe psychiatric or medical conditions that could be exacerbated by participation, listed as heart disease, glaucoma, history of seizures, pregnancy, psychosis, suicidality, bipolar disorder, and substance use disorders**, plus vision/hearing impairment severe enough to interfere.
- **Key findings:** All groups, including mindfulness meditation, showed significant daily reductions in state anxiety and negative affect and increases in positive affect. Breathwork combined produced a greater daily increase in **positive affect** than mindfulness meditation (p < 0.05 in the mixed-effects model), an effect the authors attribute mainly to the cyclic sighing arm, and the benefit grew with cumulative days of practice. There were **no between-group differences in state-anxiety or negative-affect change**. Breathwork, and cyclic sighing specifically, produced a significantly greater reduction in **respiratory rate** over the month than meditation (p < 0.05); the size of the respiratory-rate reduction correlated with positive-affect gains (r = −0.24, p < 0.05). **No significant changes in heart rate variability or resting heart rate** were found in any group. No sleep effects. In the debriefing questionnaire, 90% of participants reported positive experiences and **10% reported negative experiences** related to the exercises.
- **Limitations (per the paper):** Exploratory study not pre-registered as a clinical trial (retrospectively registered); remote delivery with limited monitoring of how closely participants followed instructions; adherence by self-report; findings limited to 4 weeks with no follow-up; small per-group samples, powered for the combined breathwork-vs-meditation comparison rather than between-breathing-arm comparisons.
- **Applicability to the exact WRH wording:** General concept only, with three specific mismatches. (1) *Protocol mismatch:* none of the three tested patterns is WRH's pattern. Cyclic sighing is a double nasal inhale followed by a prolonged exhale, with no counted 4-count inhale, no hold, and no 6-count exhale; box breathing uses equal inhale/hold/exhale durations individually calibrated by a CO2-tolerance test (3–4 s or 5–6 s phases) — it includes holds, but equal-ratio and individually scaled, not "in for four… hold… out for six." (2) *Population mismatch:* the trial screened out people with moderate-to-severe psychiatric conditions, suicidality, bipolar disorder, psychosis, and substance use disorders — categories that overlap substantially with a trauma-curriculum audience; WRH's scripts are delivered unscreened. (3) *Delivery mismatch:* the trial's practice was solitary, self-timed, daily over a month, with benefits for positive affect increasing with adherence over days; it did not test a single facilitator-led group delivery, an acute-distress moment, or an imperative instruction. The trial therefore does not evaluate WRH's procedure, its promised in-the-moment regulatory effect, or its safety in WRH's audience. It does establish that at least one exhale-emphasized pattern, practiced voluntarily and repeatedly by screened, largely healthy adults, can improve positive mood and lower respiratory rate relative to mindfulness meditation over a month.

### T3-S03 — Lehrer et al. 2020 (HRV biofeedback systematic review and meta-analysis)

- **Citation:** Lehrer, P., Kaur, K., Sharma, A., Shah, K., Huseby, R., Bhavsar, J., & Zhang, Y. (2020). Heart rate variability biofeedback improves emotional and physical health and performance: A systematic review and meta analysis. *Applied Psychophysiology and Biofeedback*, 45(3), 109–129. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-020-09466-z
- **Inspection depth:** Abstract and bibliographic record inspected (publisher article page and abstract text). Full text was not accessed in this search (paywalled); no full-text-only details are relied on.
- **Design / population / setting (per the abstract):** Systematic review and meta-analysis restricted to randomized controlled studies of heart rate variability biofeedback (HRVB). Of 1,868 papers initially identified, 58 met inclusion criteria. HRVB as studied is a multi-session intervention in which participants breathe at an individually determined resonance frequency (in the wider literature, approximately 4.5–6.5 breaths per minute for adults) with real-time physiological feedback — not a brief scripted group exercise.
- **Key findings (per the abstract):** A significant **small-to-moderate effect size** favoring HRVB across the symptoms and functioning outcomes analyzed. Largest effect sizes for anxiety, depression, anger, and athletic/artistic performance; **smallest effect sizes for PTSD, sleep, and quality of life**. Effects were larger against inactive than active control conditions, though significant for both. The authors describe HRVB as useful as a **complementary** treatment and state that further research is needed to confirm efficacy for particular applications. No significant differences by number of sessions or weeks in the abstract's moderator results.
- **Limitations:** Abstract-level inspection only in this file. Per the abstract, the per-condition study counts were small ("with a small number of studies for each"). The intervention class (instrumented, individualized, multi-session biofeedback training) differs materially from WRH's delivery (no feedback device, no individual resonance-frequency determination, no training course).
- **Applicability to the exact WRH wording:** General concept only. This source supports the proposition that paced breathing near an individual's resonance frequency, delivered as a trained biofeedback intervention, has small-to-moderate effects on a range of symptoms — with the **weakest** recorded effects for PTSD, the condition closest to WRH's audience. It does not test a fixed 4-count/hold/6-count pattern, a fixed rate for everyone, a one-off or session-opening delivery, group delivery without feedback, mandatory participation, or use during acute distress. Phase 2 had omitted HRV-biofeedback ranking claims because the primary source had not been independently inspected; this file inspects the abstract only, so no ranking claims beyond the abstract's own wording are carried forward.

### T3-S04 — Leyro et al. 2021 (respiratory interventions for anxiety: meta-analysis of RCTs)

- **Citation:** Leyro, T. M., Versella, M. V., Yang, M.-J., Brinkman, H. R., Hoyt, D. L., & Lehrer, P. (2021). Respiratory therapy for the treatment of anxiety: Meta-analytic review and regression. *Clinical Psychology Review*, 84, 101980. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2021.101980 · PMID: 33540222 · PMCID: PMC8302658
- **Inspection depth:** Full text inspected — abstract, introduction (including the hyperventilation/anxiety-sensitivity rationale), methods, results, risk-of-bias section, and discussion/limitations.
- **Design / population / setting:** Meta-analysis of **40 randomized controlled trials** of respiratory-focused interventions (diaphragmatic breathing, controlled/paced breathing, breathing retraining, HRV biofeedback, and capnometry/PaCO2 biofeedback) against non-respiratory control groups (active or inactive), with trait anxiety as an outcome. Populations spanned non-clinical, medical, and psychiatric samples; interventions were structured treatments delivered over courses ranging from 1 to 20 weeks / 1–28 sessions, not single session-opening exercises.
- **Key findings:** Respiratory interventions produced significantly greater improvement in trait anxiety than controls, described as a **moderate-to-large effect overall, with stronger effects against inactive controls; against active controls only, effects were small**. Dimensional anxiety symptoms showed the most promising effects. For **posttraumatic stress symptoms specifically, effects were small and non-significant**. The authors note, as contrary/contextual evidence, that dismantling studies of CBT for panic (citing Pompoli et al. 2018; Schmidt et al. 2000) found that **adding respiratory interventions did not improve clinical outcomes**, despite acceptability. No significant moderation by treatment duration, session count, or modality (including biofeedback vs. non-biofeedback) was found.
- **Limitations (per the paper):** A **majority of included studies were classified as high risk of bias**, many because of inactive control comparisons contributing to outcome-measurement bias; significant heterogeneity across intervention designs, populations, and controls. The authors call for active comparators matched on dose, pre-registration, and standardization. The meta-analysis did not systematically report adverse events; the word does not appear in the inspected text as an outcome category.
- **Safety-relevant findings (per the paper's discussion):** The authors record two mechanisms by which respiratory interventions can initially worsen the target state: (1) HRV biofeedback "requires individuals to dramatically slow their breathing pace, which, while increasing relaxation when done correctly, **may initially result in worry that one is not getting enough oxygen, and therefore promote anxiety**"; (2) "many respiratory interventions promote increased awareness of respiration and other related physiological changes, **which may increase sensitivity to sensations, and initially increase anxiety**." The introduction also documents, as background, that hyperventilation (PaCO2 below 30 mmHg / hypocapnia) is a core feature of panic symptomatology and that anxiety sensitivity — fear of bodily sensations, an established risk factor in panic disorder and PTSD — can magnify the salience of respiratory symptoms.
- **Applicability to the exact WRH wording:** This is the strongest inspected source for the general concept that structured respiratory interventions can reduce anxiety symptoms over a treatment course, and simultaneously the clearest inspected source against extrapolating that concept to WRH's use: (1) the outcome WRH's audience most resembles — posttraumatic stress symptoms — showed **small, non-significant** effects; (2) effects shrink to **small** against active controls, and most underlying trials were high risk of bias; (3) the studied interventions were multi-session treatments with defined protocols, not a brief opener repeated at every session; (4) the paper documents initial-anxiety-increase mechanisms that are directly relevant to delivering slowed breathing to unscreened trauma participants, including participants with panic features. It does not evaluate WRH's exact pattern, its imperative delivery, or its use in acute distress.

### T3-S05 — Szulczewski 2019 (paced breathing at 0.1 Hz: CO2 homeostasis, hyperventilation symptoms, and training)

- **Citation:** Szulczewski, M. T. (2019). Training of paced breathing at 0.1 Hz improves CO2 homeostasis and relaxation during a paced breathing task. *PLoS ONE*, 14(6), e0218550. PMCID: PMC6586331 · PMID: 31220170 · Stable URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6586331/
- **Inspection depth:** Full text inspected — abstract, introduction, methods (participants, apparatus, procedure), and results summary; discussion sections beyond the results opening were not relied on for detail.
- **Design / population / setting:** Single-group training study. **16 participants** (8 men, 8 women), aged 19–29, recruited via social media, performed **10 minutes of paced breathing at 0.1 Hz (6 breaths/minute)** daily for 7 days, with laboratory capnography (end-tidal CO2, PetCO2), hyperventilation-symptom ratings, and affect ratings on days 1, 4, and 7. The pacing used **4-second inhalation and 6-second exhalation** — the same inhale/exhale durations as WRH's counts if counts are seconds, but **with no hold** and delivered by an audiovisual pacer. **Exclusion criteria (self-report): chronic respiratory diseases (e.g., asthma), other chronic diseases (e.g., diabetes), cardiovascular diseases, psychiatric and neurological conditions**, and any condition affecting nasal breathing; people with prior breathing training (e.g., yoga, singing) were also excluded. No anti-hyperventilation instructions (such as "breathe shallower") were given — matching WRH's scripts, which likewise give no depth instruction.
- **Key findings:** (1) On day 1, paced breathing significantly **dropped PetCO2**, and **37.5% of participants (6 of 16) fell below 30 mmHg** — the hypocapnia threshold the paper treats as hyperventilation. By day 7, the PetCO2 drop had significantly decreased and no participant fell below 30 mmHg (except one already below at baseline). (2) Paced breathing produced **hyperventilation symptoms of mild intensity which did not decrease with training** — measured symptoms were dizziness, tingling/pricking, numbness, headache, and increased muscle tension in hands and feet. (3) Affect shifted toward calmness and relaxation **only on the second and third laboratory measurements, not on the first**, and the task was rated more pleasant on later days. The author's conclusion: "training paced breathing is necessary to make the task more pleasant and relaxing." The introduction records the proposed mechanism — that during paced breathing, untrained participants tend to hyperventilate because volitional control overrides compensatory mechanisms — and lists the undesirable effects of a large CO2 drop: increased heart rate, paresthesia and tetany, dizziness, lightheadedness, and increased emotional arousal. It also reports, from a cited 9-week slow-paced-breathing trial for hot flashes, adverse effects of dizziness, palpitations, and tingling in extremities, and notes that in paced-breathing training generally "often neither capnography measurement nor an anti-hyperventilation instruction is used," and that in mobile apps "the issue of potential hyperventilation is frequently overlooked."
- **Limitations:** Very small sample (n=16), young screened healthy adults, single-group design without a control condition; symptom intensity was mild; the study was not conducted in a clinical, group, or distress setting.
- **Applicability to the exact WRH wording:** This is the closest inspected analogue to WRH's counted pattern (4 in / 6 out) and the most directly contrary evidence to an assumption of immediate, universal benefit: in screened, healthy, untrained young adults, the **first** performance of this pacing produced measurable hyperventilation in over a third of participants, mild hyperventilation symptoms that persisted across the week, and **no** calming affect shift — the relaxing/pleasant effects appeared only after repeated daily practice. WRH's Anchor is delivered precisely at first performance and repeatedly thereafter to an **unscreened** trauma audience (this study excluded respiratory, cardiovascular, and psychiatric conditions; WRH's scripts include no screening), with an added unspecified hold, no depth instruction, no CO2 monitoring, and an imperative delivery. The study does not test WRH's script, group delivery, or a distressed population, and its mild, transient symptoms in healthy volunteers do not establish the size of any risk in WRH's population — but it removes any basis for assuming the counted pattern is physiologically neutral or immediately calming for everyone on first use.

### T3-S06 — Taylor 2001 (breathing retraining in panic disorder: efficacy, caveats, indications)

- **Citation:** Taylor, S. (2001). Breathing retraining in the treatment of panic disorder: Efficacy, caveats and indications. *Scandinavian Journal of Behaviour Therapy*, 30(2), 49–56. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/02845710118895
- **Inspection depth:** Abstract only (publisher/PsycNet record). Full text was not accessed; no full-text-only details are relied on.
- **Design / population / setting:** Invited narrative review of breathing retraining as used in the treatment of **panic disorder** — i.e., clinician-delivered treatment for a diagnosed population, in which retraining typically means teaching slow, diaphragmatic breathing to counter hyperventilation/chest breathing.
- **Key findings (per the abstract):** Several studies suggest breathing retraining reduces panic frequency, but the review raises two caveats against routine use: (1) research suggests **hyperventilation plays a limited role in producing panic attacks**, so retraining "may only be useful for a minority of patients, for whom hyperventilation (or chest breathing) plays a role in producing panic symptoms"; (2) retraining "may be **counterproductive** to the extent that it prevents patients from learning that their catastrophic beliefs are unfounded" — i.e., it can function as escape/avoidance of feared sensations. Conclusion: retraining can play a useful role, but "clinicians must exercise care to ensure that it is not misused by patients as a means of escaping or avoiding feared sensations."
- **Limitations:** Abstract-only inspection; the review is from 2001 and predates the meta-analyses above (T3-S03, T3-S04), which are the better guide to current effect-size estimates. It concerns diagnosed panic disorder treatment, not group psychoeducation.
- **Applicability to the exact WRH wording:** Indirect but relevant to the framing question. WRH's Anchor and "reset" language (ER-249/ER-257: the straw-exhale maneuver is "your reset" for when the system "starts to get loud") positions breathing as the tool to deploy against rising arousal sensations. Taylor's caveat — recorded here as a clinician-review hypothesis from the panic literature, not as a finding about WRH — is that assigning breathing that escape/avoidance role is the specific misuse pattern clinicians are warned about in panic treatment. Whether that pattern applies to WRH's use is a judgment for the qualified reviewer; this source does not evaluate WRH, group delivery, or non-panic populations.

### T3-S07 — Fincham et al. 2023 (breathwork and mental health: meta-analysis of RCTs)

- **Citation:** Fincham, G. W., Strauss, C., Montero-Marin, J., & Cavanagh, K. (2023). Effect of breathwork on stress and mental health: A meta-analysis of randomised-controlled trials. *Scientific Reports*, 13, 432. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y
- **Inspection depth:** Abstract and selected results/discussion text inspected (publisher PDF text), including the adverse-events reporting passage and the primary stress analysis. Full methods and all subgroup analyses were not inspected.
- **Design / population / setting:** Meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials of breathwork (deliberate control of the breath, slow- and fast-paced) vs. non-breathwork controls in adults. Primary stress outcome: **k=12 RCTs, 785 participants**. Secondary outcomes: anxiety (k=20), depressive symptoms (k=18). Most included studies were rated at moderate risk of bias.
- **Key findings:** Breathwork was associated with significantly lower self-reported stress than controls post-intervention, **g = −0.35** [95% CI −0.55, −0.14], p = 0.0009 (small-to-medium); anxiety **g = −0.32**, p < 0.0001; depressive symptoms **g = −0.40**, p < 0.0001, with moderate and significant heterogeneity for the secondary outcomes. There were insufficient follow-up studies to meta-analyze durability. The type of breathwork (slow vs. fast), delivery method (individual vs. group, remote vs. in-person), and population (clinical vs. non-clinical) did **not** significantly moderate the stress effect. The authors urge caution and warn against "a miscalibration between hype and evidence."
- **Safety reporting (per the inspected text):** "We did not find reported adverse events or lasting bad effects directly attributed to breathwork interventions; **four studies (six in total including secondary outcome studies) actively reported on this**." That is, adverse-event monitoring was actively reported in only a small minority of the underlying trials; the absence of reported harms in the rest is an absence of reporting, not a demonstrated safety finding. One included fast-paced-breathwork study in unipolar/bipolar depression reported side effects (hot flushes, shortness of breath, sweating) in a small subgroup; no participant dropped out of that study's breathwork group due to adverse effects.
- **Limitations:** Outcomes were self-reported/subjective; most studies at moderate risk of bias; insufficient follow-up data; the pooled "breathwork" category mixes techniques (including fast-paced practices unlike WRH's) and multi-session programs, so the pooled effect does not describe any single protocol.
- **Applicability to the exact WRH wording:** General concept only. The pooled small-to-medium self-report effects describe structured breathwork programs vs. mostly non-breathwork controls, not a brief counted opener, not an acute-distress intervention, and not WRH's population or imperative delivery. For the safety question, this source establishes the state of the record: **harms monitoring in breathwork RCTs is sparse**, so the trial literature cannot be cited as showing that scripted breathing delivered to unscreened groups is safe; it mostly does not look.

## 3. Verdict

### 3.1 What the evidence establishes

1. **Slow, paced breathing has real, measurable physiological effects in adults, and structured breathing interventions produce small-to-moderate improvements in stress and anxiety outcomes in the populations studied.** Converging inspected sources: slow breathing is associated with physiological and psychological changes in healthy subjects (T3-S01, within Phase 2's recorded limits); breathing near an individual resonance frequency as HRV biofeedback shows small-to-moderate pooled effects across conditions (T3-S03, abstract); respiratory interventions beat controls on trait anxiety, moderate-to-large against inactive controls but **small against active controls**, with most underlying trials at high risk of bias (T3-S04); pooled breathwork effects on self-reported stress/anxiety/depression are small-to-medium (g ≈ −0.35 / −0.32 / −0.40) (T3-S07); a month of daily 5-minute exhale-emphasized breathwork improved positive affect and lowered respiratory rate more than mindfulness meditation in screened, largely healthy adults, with **no** HRV or resting-heart-rate change (T3-S02).
2. **The effects are practice- and course-based, not demonstrated single-use effects.** The clearest dose picture in the inspected set: Balban's benefits grew with cumulative days of daily practice over 28 days (T3-S02), and Szulczewski's calming/pleasantness effects were absent at first performance and appeared on later measurement days (T3-S05). No inspected source demonstrates that a first, brief, facilitator-led delivery produces immediate regulation in the moment it is delivered.
3. **Documented cautions exist for exactly the failure mode relevant to an unscreened trauma group.** Inspected sources record that: untrained people commonly hyperventilate during paced breathing — at WRH's own inhale/exhale durations (4 s / 6 s, no hold), 37.5% of screened healthy young adults dropped below the hypocapnia threshold on day 1 and mild hyperventilation symptoms (dizziness, tingling, numbness, headache, muscle tension) persisted across a week of practice (T3-S05); dramatically slowing the breathing pace can initially provoke air-hunger worry and thereby **increase** anxiety, and heightened awareness of respiratory sensations can initially increase anxiety (T3-S04); in the panic-disorder literature, breathing retraining is cautioned against routine, unconsidered use because hyperventilation drives panic for only a minority and because using breathing to escape feared sensations can be counterproductive (T3-S06, abstract). These are cautions documented in clinical/research sources, not proof that WRH's scripts have harmed anyone; no inspected source reports on WRH at all.
4. **The trials that support breathing interventions protected their participants in ways WRH's scripts do not.** Balban excluded moderate-to-severe psychiatric and several medical conditions, suicidality, and substance use disorders, and still recorded negative experiences in 10% of participants (T3-S02). Szulczewski excluded respiratory, cardiovascular, and psychiatric conditions (T3-S05). HRV biofeedback individualizes the breathing rate and uses physiological feedback (T3-S03). The Anchor scripts apply one fixed pattern, with an unspecified hold and no depth instruction, to an unscreened room.
5. **Authoritative PTSD guidance does not validate this class of practice as treatment, and frames comparable practices as options, not mandates.** The 2023 VA/DoD PTSD guideline's evidence base, as summarized by the National Center for PTSD, gave no CIH practice a "strong for" recommendation, does not support CIH as first-line PTSD treatment, and describes CIH use as appropriate for patients who **prefer** those options, within a care plan whose goals are evaluated; trauma-sensitive delivery of body-based practice is associated in that summary with **invitatory language** (T3-S08). SAMHSA's trauma-informed framework is principle-based — safety; empowerment, voice, and choice; collaboration and mutuality; resisting re-traumatization — and expressly not a prescribed set of procedures (T3-S09).

### 3.2 What the evidence does not establish

1. **It does not establish that WRH's specific protocol works.** No inspected source tests the Anchor scripts, the "in for four… hold… out for six" pattern with its unspecified hold, the feet/hands placement combination, the straw-exhale "reset," or the sensory grounding scripts (ER-101, ER-135), in any population. General slow-breathing evidence — the only kind located — cannot be transferred to the exact wording, procedure, audience, or implied outcomes without a gap the reviewer must acknowledge rather than bridge silently. This is the same boundary Phase 2 recorded for ER-011/RGP, and nothing inspected here moves it.
2. **It does not establish rapid or acute-distress efficacy.** ER-011's framing ("immediate intervention during moments of participant dysregulation") has a T3 analogue in the Anchor/"reset" framing (use when the system "lights up" or "starts to get loud"). No inspected source tests paced breathing delivered during acute distress, panic, or dissociation, and the two most situation-specific findings point the other way for first use: first-session paced breathing produced hyperventilation and no calming shift in healthy volunteers (T3-S05), and slowed breathing can initially raise anxiety in sensation-sensitive people (T3-S04).
3. **It does not establish benefit for posttraumatic stress symptoms.** The one inspected meta-analytic estimate for posttraumatic stress symptoms was **small and non-significant** (T3-S04), and the HRV-biofeedback meta-analysis recorded its **smallest** effects for PTSD (T3-S03, abstract). A trauma audience is the population for which the inspected breathing evidence is weakest, not strongest.
4. **It does not establish safety for mandatory, unscreened, group delivery.** Safety for that delivery mode is not affirmatively disproven either — no inspected source studies it. What is established is narrower: harms monitoring in breathwork trials is sparse (T3-S07), the supportive trials screened out higher-risk participants (T3-S02, T3-S05), and plausible, documented mechanisms exist by which the instruction can make some participants feel worse in the short term (T3-S04, T3-S05, T3-S06). "No reported adverse events" in this literature is a reporting artifact and must not be quoted as a safety finding.
5. **It does not establish that the traffic-light opt-out resolves the optionality question.** The opt-out language and the imperative breathing instruction coexist in the same Anchor (ER-023/ER-278/ER-332; §1.1). No inspected source evaluates whether an opt-out framed around leaving/stepping out makes a simultaneously delivered "Do it" instruction effectively voluntary in a group setting. That is a delivery-design and clinical judgment question (the PCC-011 question), not an empirical one this research can settle.

### 3.3 What is unresolved

1. **The PCC-011 question, extended to T3 (the backlog's stated review question):** what role the Anchor scripts should have (opening ritual, optional tool, or intervention), what optionality standard applies to a scripted instruction delivered to a whole room, what contraindication screening (if any) is proportionate — respiratory conditions (e.g., asthma/COPD), panic/hyperventilation sensitivity, and cardiovascular conditions are the categories the inspected trials themselves screened for (T3-S02, T3-S05) — and what escalation boundary applies when a participant becomes more distressed during or after the Anchor. **Unresolved; requires a qualified clinician and site-implementation review.** No inspected source answers it.
2. **Whether the hold matters.** WRH's pattern includes a hold of unspecified duration; the closest physiological analogue inspected (T3-S05) used no hold, and Balban's hold-bearing arm (box breathing) used equal, individually calibrated phases (T3-S02). The effect and tolerability of WRH's unspecified hold are unstudied in the inspected literature. Also unstudied: the script's silence on breath depth, which is the variable that produced hyperventilation in T3-S05.
3. **Whether repeated delivery changes the risk/benefit picture.** Szulczewski's training effect (hyperventilation resolving, calm appearing by day 4–7 of daily practice) and Balban's adherence effect cut both ways for a curriculum that repeats the Anchor at every session: later deliveries occur in practiced participants, but every new participant's first deliveries carry the first-use profile, and the scripts are also positioned for in-the-moment distress use, which is the untested case (§3.2.2). How a reviewer should weigh this is unresolved on the inspected evidence.
4. **Grounding variants.** For ER-101, ER-135, ER-249/ER-257, and ER-784, support was not located in this search (§2.1). Their implied effects ("teach your system it's safe to stay," "bring you back into the room," "your reset") remain unexamined against primary sources. Unresolved, not negatived.
5. **ISTSS and other guideline bodies on stabilization-phase instructions.** Not located in this search (§2.1); a reviewer with guideline access should confirm whether any authoritative trauma guidance addresses scripted breathing/grounding instructions, their framing (mandatory vs. invitational), or screening, before the optionality question is closed.

### 3.4 Bottom line for the family

The inspected evidence supports a general concept — slow, structured breathing, practiced voluntarily over time by screened or self-selected adults, can modestly reduce stress and anxiety and measurably slow respiration — and it does not support the specific thing the T3 scripts do: deliver one fixed counted pattern, imperatively, to an unscreened trauma group as a standing opener and in-the-moment "reset," with an implied regulatory effect. For the population WRH serves, the inspected effect estimates are the weakest in the set (posttraumatic stress symptoms: small and non-significant, T3-S04; smallest HRVB effects for PTSD, T3-S03), the documented cautions (first-use hyperventilation, initial anxiety increase, avoidance-function risk) are specific and relevant, and no authoritative guidance inspected supplies validation, a mandatory framing, or a screening/escalation framework for these scripts. The disposition questions — role, optionality, screening, escalation — remain open and belong to qualified clinical review. This file proposes no replacement wording, and none for any crisis-adjacent passage.

## 3. The specific mechanistic claim, decomposed

WRH's sentence bundles three distinct assertions. They have different evidence states and must not be traded for one another:

### T1 — Torre & Lieberman (2018) — the Phase 2 candidate lead, inspected in this batch

- **Full citation:** Torre, J. B., & Lieberman, M. D. (2018). Putting feelings into words: Affect labeling as implicit emotion regulation. *Emotion Review*, 10(2), 116–124. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/1754073917742706
- **Inspection depth:** **Full text inspected in this batch** — the complete article text (abstract, experiential / autonomic / neural / behavioral sections, "Possible Mechanisms" section, "Further Considerations" section, conclusions, and references) as rendered from a PDF copy of the published article hosted on Academia.edu (`https://www.academia.edu/43375697/Emotions`). Bibliographic identity (authors, journal, year, DOI, pagination as cited in secondary records) is consistent across that copy and the publisher/DeepDyve record located in search. This is recorded honestly as a full-text copy inspection, not a publisher-site inspection.
- **Study design:** Narrative **review** (invited special-section article), not an experiment. It has no participants of its own. It compares affect labeling with reappraisal across four domains and then, in its own words, *speculates* on mechanisms.
- **Population / setting (of the evidence it reviews):** Predominantly laboratory studies in which participants label emotional faces, emotionally evocative images/objects, or their own feelings during image viewing, plus the exposure-therapy studies it cites (spider fear / arachnophobia, public-speaking anxiety). Settings are scanners, labs, and structured exposure sessions — not group psychoeducation, and not people in acute crisis or panic.
- **Key findings as the review states them:**
  - Affect labeling "can attenuate our emotional experiences" and produces "a pattern of effects like those seen during explicit emotion regulation," leading the authors to characterize it as a form of *implicit* emotion regulation — i.e., it need not feel like regulating, and participants typically predict it will *increase* their distress even after experiencing reduced distress in the task.
  - Experiential: labeling (one's own state, others' states, or evocative objects) tended to reduce self-reported distress to aversive images vs. non-labeling conditions; it also reduced pleasant feelings to positive images — attenuation of affect, not selective relief of negative affect.
  - Autonomic: both immediate reductions (e.g., heart-rate / skin-conductance measures in some paradigms) and **delayed-only** effects (see M3 above; e.g., reduced responding 48 hours, 1 week, or 8 days later in film / spider-image / exposure paradigms).
  - Neural: increased vlPFC and decreased amygdala activity vs. control conditions that process the same stimulus without labeling affect (including gender labeling); negative vlPFC–amygdala connectivity; a dynamic-causal-modeling result (Torrisi et al., cited therein) interpreted as vlPFC output driving decreased amygdala activity.
  - Behavioral: greater approach progress in spider exposure for the labeling condition (Kircanski et al. 2012, = S07), and other downstream effects the review lists.
- **Limitations the review itself states (directly relevant here):**
  - **Mechanisms have not been directly investigated.** Verbatim sense of the section opening: mechanisms of affect labeling "have not yet been directly investigated and as such our understanding of the critical processes involved remains a matter of speculation." The candidate mechanisms it then outlines — distraction, self-reflection, reduction of uncertainty, symbolic conversion — are presented as candidates, not findings. WRH's sentence asserts a settled mechanism ("gives your prefrontal cortex…"); the review that WRH's evidence chain leads to explicitly says the mechanism is unsettled.
  - Provided-label vs. self-generated-label timing dichotomy (see M3), whose reason "remains unknown and, as of yet, untested."
  - Open boundary conditions the review flags as unanswered: whether there is such a thing as overuse of labeling (one cited result suggests a curvilinear relationship — some label use beneficial, too much detrimental); how affect **intensity** affects labeling success (in the reappraisal literature people prefer other strategies at high intensity — the review asks whether the same holds for labeling, which is precisely WRH's use case); differences between labeling one's own feelings, others' feelings, and evocative objects ("largely unknown"); and individual differences such as emotional granularity.
  - At least one null / reversed instance is noted in the experiential section ("this may not always be the case," citing Matejka et al.), and the sole reported case of labeling *increasing* initial arousal involved self-generated verbalization.
  - As a review authored by the originators of the affect-labeling paradigm (Lieberman is senior author of S06 and S07), it is an author exposition of that research program, not an independent evaluation of it.
- **Applicability to WRH's exact wording:** Supports, at most, the general direction behind M1 (labeling can engage RVLPFC and attenuate affect under laboratory task conditions). It does **not** mention, measure, or support "air" (M2), a one-second figure (M3), naming a *pattern/wiring/state* as opposed to labeling an emotion or stimulus, use by a person in panic or suicidal crisis, use as a facilitator-delivered closing tool in a group curriculum, or any of the session-specific outcomes the variants promise. Its clinical-population evidence is confined to specific-phobia / speaking-anxiety exposure, with effects measured at follow-up, not in the moment of acute distress.
- **General concept vs. WRH's procedure:** Evaluates the **general concept** of affect labeling only. It does not evaluate WRH, WRH's naming procedure, WRH's audience, or WRH's promised outcomes.

## 6. Verdict

### 6.1 What the evidence establishes

1. **A general concept has laboratory support, in narrower form than WRH states it.** Under specific laboratory task conditions, putting feelings into words (affect labeling) is associated with increased activity in right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex and decreased amygdala activity relative to non-labeling control tasks, and with attenuation of self-reported affect and some autonomic measures (S06 abstract-only; S07 full text for the exposure adjunct finding; synthesized in T1, full text). This is support for a *direction of inquiry* about labeling and prefrontal–amygdala dynamics — not for WRH's sentence.
2. **In one clinical-adjacent paradigm, labeling during exposure was followed by a physiological/behavioral benefit at follow-up** (S07: skin conductance and approach progress at one week in spider-fearful participants), while self-reported fear showed no corresponding between-group benefit in the same study. Channels dissociate; "it worked" is not a channel-free statement.
3. **WRH's refrain is curriculum-wide, not a two-session wording.** 53 Phase 3 wording entries / 139 entry-location records / 72 distinct literal phrase positions, in at least five continuation forms (§2.3), plus the two Phase 2 entries. Any reviewer disposition on ER-005/ER-014 that does not state whether it covers these locations leaves most of the family undisposed.
4. **WRH's own text is internally inconsistent on the family's central number.** Session 50 (ER-490) asserts "one second of air" and, in the same sentence, a three-second logging period. At least one of the two figures is not a measurement.

### 6.2 What the evidence does not establish

1. **The mechanism as stated (M2).** No inspected source measures or reports prefrontal "air," oxygenation, or any equivalent quantity produced by naming. The word has no measured referent in this evidence base.
2. **The timing as stated (M3).** No inspected source reports a one-second latency, duration, or decision window from labeling. The timing evidence located (T1) describes immediate effects for *provided* labels and delayed effects (days) — with an initial *increase* in arousal in one paradigm — for *self-generated* labels. WRH's procedure is self-generated.
3. **That the mechanism is settled science.** The review WRH's chain leads to states the mechanisms "have not yet been directly investigated" and "remains a matter of speculation" (T1), and a 2026 replication paper characterizes the mechanisms as unclear with contradictory results (T4). WRH's sentence presents mechanism + magnitude as established fact; the literature presents both as open.
4. **Effectiveness — or safety — in panic or acute crisis.** No inspected source studies a person in panic, in suicidal crisis, or in acute distress using naming as WRH prescribes it. Phase 2's conclusion stands unchanged: untested in crisis or panic.
5. **That naming first is reliably beneficial even in mild conditions.** Three inspected contrary sources (T2, N = 80 + 60; T3, N = 100 across the adult age span; T4, N = 226) found naming *before* an explicit regulation strategy made that regulation **less** effective on self-reported affect. These do not show naming alone is harmful, and they are image-paradigm studies — but they defeat any assumption that the naming → regulation sequence is unconditionally facilitative, which is the assumption WRH's Part II variants ("One second to… use the regulation tools…") depend on.
6. **Any session-specific promised outcome** in the variant continuations (decoupling value from work, interrupting shutdown, "the sovereignty," "the integration," etc.). No inspected source addresses any of them.
7. **Anything about WRH itself.** No inspected source evaluates WRH, its sessions, its facilitators, or its participants.

### 6.3 What remains unresolved

1. Whether *any* form of naming/labeling, delivered as WRH delivers it (self-generated, group setting, facilitator script, closing tool), produces a measurable in-the-moment benefit for a person in acute distress — no located study tests this configuration at all.
2. Which candidate mechanism, if any, drives laboratory affect-labeling effects (distraction, self-reflection, uncertainty reduction, symbolic conversion — all listed as speculative in T1), and whether any of them would predict WRH's stated prefrontal-"air" pathway.
3. The boundary conditions T1 flags as unanswered and WRH does not acknowledge: effect of affect **intensity** on labeling success; possible detriment from overuse of labels (curvilinear result cited in T1); differences between labeling one's own feelings vs. a stimulus vs. a pattern; and moderation by emotional granularity.
4. Whether the contrary naming-before-regulation result (T2–T4) replicates in clinical or high-intensity samples, and whether it extends from reappraisal/acceptance sequences to WRH's "name it, then use a regulation tool" sequence specifically.
5. Whether the Session 50 "three seconds" figure reflects a second, distinct claim somewhere else in the curriculum that a future inventory pass should register separately — not investigated in this batch.
6. Independent reviewer disposition for the entire family (all reviewer fields remain PENDING), including the explicit cover-or-exclude decision for the 53 Phase 3 entries relative to ER-005/ER-014.

- **Full citation:** Bechara, A., & Damasio, A. R. (2005). The somatic marker hypothesis: A neural theory of economic decision. *Games and Economic Behavior*, 52(2), 336–372.
- **DOI / stable URL:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geb.2004.06.010
- **Inspection depth (this worker, 2026-10-20):** Full text accessible (publisher-identical article copy). **Inspected:** abstract, Introduction, §1 "The somatic marker hypothesis" (including §1.1 induction of somatic states, §1.2, §1.3.1 body loop, §1.3.2 as-if body loop), and §2–§2.2 (gambling-task testing, anticipatory skin-conductance results). - **Findings (from the inspected sections):**
  - The hypothesis is a framework **for decision-making**: decision-making "is influenced by marker signals that arise in bioregulatory processes, including those that express themselves in emotions and feelings," operating consciously or non-consciously.

- **Applicability to WRH's exact wording:** Establishes what the established term "somatic marker" means in its home literature (see §3). ## 3. The Damasio comparison

**What "somatic marker" means in its coined usage** (per D01, corroborated by D02's abstract/§1): In the somatic marker hypothesis (Damasio and colleagues, 1991; Damasio, 1994; exposition in Bechara & Damasio, 2005), a somatic marker is an **emotion-derived bodily signal** — a somatic state, or its brain-simulated "as-if" counterpart — that is attached to the **anticipated consequences of a contemplated action** and **biases the choice among options** when deciding under complexity or uncertainty. Its reference points are specific: eliciting inducers (primary/secondary), integration in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, and an operational measure (anticipatory skin conductance) in a decision task. A marker *points forward*: from past emotional experience toward a future outcome of a decision not yet taken.

**What WRH's sentence does:** "[Symptom list] are all somatic markers of this law" labels **currently experienced symptoms, behaviors, cognitions, beliefs, and (in Sessions 25–26) positive states** as evidence that a curriculum "law" (e.g., "Necessary Deconstruction," Law #20) is operating in the participant. The marker *points backward/inward*: from a present experience to membership in the curriculum's explanatory scheme, including — in Session 20 — the implication that the experience signals a healing process underway.

**Where the analogy breaks:**

1. **Different relata.** Damasio's marker relates a bodily signal to a *prospective consequence of an option*. WRH's marker relates a symptom to a *law*. There is no decision, no option set, and no prospective outcome in WRH's sentence.
2. **Different direction of inference.** SMH: past emotional learning → anticipatory signal → advantageous choice. WRH: present distress → diagnosis that a named law is at work (and, in Session 20, that deconstruction precedes rebirth). Nothing in D01/D02 licenses the second inference.
3. **Not restricted to the somatic.** Several WRH lists consist partly or wholly of non-bodily items — beliefs ("you are fundamentally flawed"), thoughts ("obsessive thinking about 'what if'"), capacities ("the ability to help others"). In Damasio's usage, "somatic" is the defining feature (body-related responses hallmarking an emotion). WRH's usage empties the qualifier of content.
4. **No operational or neural content.** WRH offers no inducer structure, no VMPFC/integration claim, no measurable signal. The term appears as a label only.
5. **Different speech act.** Damasio's construct is a (contested — D02) scientific hypothesis about mechanism. WRH's sentence is delivered in a facilitator script as a diagnostic assertion to participants about their own current state.
6. **Risk asymmetry.** The one place the borrowing matters most is the place it fits least: Damasio's markers exist to steer a decider *away* from disadvantageous options; ER-001 uses the term to reframe suicidal ideation — in S01's terms a risk state to be assessed and managed — as a sign of a healing law.

**Conclusion of the comparison (bounded):** WRH's usage does not correspond to Damasio's construct; it borrows an established neuroscience term for a different claim (symptom-as-evidence-of-a-curriculum-law). This is a terminological finding from the inspected sources, not a finding that the borrowing was deliberate — **no inspected WRH text cites Damasio, and the intent behind the borrowing is unestablished**. It is also not, by itself, a finding that the WRH sentences are clinically unsafe; that judgment belongs to the qualified review framed in §5. Note also that even had WRH used the term correctly, D02 records that the hypothesis's evidentiary status is disputed in its home field, so the term could not have functioned as settled-science authority in any case.

## 5. Verdict

### What the evidence establishes

1. **The pattern is real, templated, and curriculum-wide.** 21 Phase 3 register entries (22 locations, ER-100 doubled across Sessions 10/14) plus ER-001 use the identical sentence "…are all somatic markers of this law" in the Mechanism segment, verified against the session source files. The blocking question raised by ER-001 therefore cannot be dispositioned as a Session 20 one-off; the backlog's instruction stands — a clinician's disposition should state whether it covers the whole family.
2. **The lists exceed the word "somatic."** The labeled items include beliefs, thoughts, behaviors, affects, and positive states, not only bodily sensations (§1.3, samples in §1.2). Whatever the sentences mean, they do not mean what "somatic" ordinarily restricts them to.
3. **"Somatic marker" is an established technical term with a different, specific meaning.** In Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis, as expounded by Bechara & Damasio (2005) and independently summarized by Dunn, Dalgleish & Lawrence (2006), somatic markers are emotion-based biasing signals that regulate **decision-making** under uncertainty by marking prospective consequences of options. WRH's usage — present symptoms as markers of a curriculum law — does not correspond to that construct (§3). The term is borrowed; the claim is different.
4. **The safe-messaging constraint behind the blocking question is grounded.** SPRC guidance (S02, as inspected in Phase 2) favors hopeful, help-seeking suicide communication and cautions against romanticizing death; VA/DoD guidance (S01) frames suicidal ideation as a risk state for assessment and management. These sources establish *why ER-001 requires qualified clinical review*. That is the full extent of their role.

### What the evidence does **not** establish

1. **It does not establish that any T5 sentence is clinically harmful or clinically safe.** No inspected source tests WRH, Session 20's exercise, or the effect of this reframe on participants. S01/S02 are guidance, not experiments on this framing — Phase 2 said the same of ER-001, and it remains true for the family.
2. **It does not establish that the family is physiologically false.** Neither S01/S02 (messaging/risk guidance) nor D01/D02 (decision-making neuroscience) is a test of whether the listed experiences occur, co-occur, or relate to trauma processes. "Support not located in this search" for the exact "markers of this law" claim is recorded; it is not a disproof, and no source located in this task purports to validate the claim either.
3. **It does not establish that WRH borrowed the term from Damasio deliberately, or at all as a conscious citation.** No WRH text inspected cites Damasio; the correspondence failure in §3 is terminological, and intent is unestablished.
4. **It does not validate Damasio's hypothesis itself.** D02 records substantive criticism of the SMH's evidence base; D01 is the proponents' own exposition. Neither is used here as settled authority for anything beyond what the term means.
5. Nothing here evaluates WRH's efficacy, and no general finding about trauma, dissociation, burnout, shame, or growth in the cited sources may be read as support for WRH's exact wording, audience, procedure, or promised outcomes.

### What remains unresolved

1. The clinical disposition itself (see questions below) — reviewer fields for ER-001 and all 21 Phase 3 entries remain **PENDING**; no qualified reviewer has been recorded for this family.
2. Whether a disposition covers the variants: ER-036 (singular) and the 19 Part III "of this session" entries (§1.3, §4).
3. Whether the Session 20 co-text (ER-002 "biological necessity," ER-003 "rebirth" promise, the Deconstruction Loop) changes the assessment of ER-001 relative to the same sentence pattern elsewhere — i.e., whether risk, if any, is a property of the sentence pattern, of the Session 20 framing, or of the combination.
4. The tier placement of ER-056 ("physical collapse after a project") and any other borderline content-adjacency calls in §4.

> **Q1 (blocking, ER-001):** For a participant in Session 20 who is currently experiencing suicidal ideation, does delivering the scripted sentence "Suicidal ideation (not necessarily intent, but a desire for the pain to stop), existential despair, and loss of meaning are all somatic markers of this law" — inside a segment that frames the crisis as "Necessary Deconstruction" and pairs it with a promise of "rebirth" — risk normalizing or romanticizing suicidal ideation, or delaying disclosure, help-seeking, or risk assessment, contrary to the safe-messaging standard reflected in SPRC guidance (S02) and the risk-assessment posture of the VA/DoD guideline (S01)? ### S-T6-1 — Roelofs (2017): human freezing as involuntary, defensive, action-preparatory

- **Full citation:** Roelofs, K. (2017). Freeze for action: Neurobiological mechanisms in animal and human freezing. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B: Biological Sciences, 372*(1718), 20160206. Published online 2017; accepted 2016 Nov 11; issue date 2017 Apr 19. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2016.0206. Stable full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5332864/ (PMCID PMC5332864, PMID 28242739). Open access (CC-BY 4.0), Royal Society.
- **Inspection depth:** FULL TEXT — abstract, §§1–9 (Introduction, Phenomenology incl. Box 1, Neural mechanisms, freezing in humans, neural correlates, individual differences, Outlook) and reference list scan; targeted `find` for "dissociat", "dorsal vagal", and "human freezing". This upgrades Phase 2's abstract-only record (S10 in `docs/PHASE2_SOURCE_AUDIT.md`) to full-text for this task.
- **Design/population:** Comparative narrative review, not a primary experiment. Synthesizes rodent/primate freezing work with human lab studies (aversive picture-viewing with the International Affective Picture System, threat-of-shock paradigms, posturography/stabilometry, ECG, fMRI).
- **Findings relevant to T6:**
  - Freezing is characterized as "a form of behavioural inhibition accompanied by parasympathetically dominated heart rate deceleration" and explicitly "not a passive state but rather a parasympathetic brake on the motor system, relevant to perception and action preparation" (Abstract).
  - Freezing is differentiated from other threat-induced immobility states: from orienting (habituation, response-preparation patterns differ) and from **tonic immobility**, which "occurs later, for example, in case of circa-strike physical contact, when fight, flight and freezing are no longer optimal for survival" and is "passive… 'playing dead'" whereas freezing "actively prepares the animal for further defensive responses" (Phenomenology §2).
  - Human evidence: threat-induced heart-rate deceleration coupled with reduced body sway on a stabilometric force platform; freezing linked to enhanced detection of coarse (low-spatial-frequency) visual information and to action preparation in go/no-go shooting paradigms (Gladwin et al.); amygdala–periaqueductal grey circuitry implicated in fMRI, with explicit cautions about limited BOLD spatial resolution (§6, §7).
- **Limitations stated by the author:** "Despite the potential relevance of freezing for human stress-coping, its phenomenology and neurobiological underpinnings remain **largely unexplored in humans**" (Abstract). Human findings come from controlled lab threat paradigms in non-clinical (or anxiety-correlated) samples — not from clinical dissociation states, not from participants receiving psychoeducation. No "dorsal vagal shutdown" terminology appears in the full text (verified by `find`). Dissociation as a clinical state is not discussed (the only "dissociat*" hit is a reference title). Nothing in the review tests or addresses the WRH claim that a participant's freeze "couldn't" have been otherwise, or that dissociation reliably engages after freeze fails.
- **Applicability to WRH's exact wording:** Supports the GENERAL CONCEPT that freezing can be an involuntary, defensive, parasympathetically dominated state relevant to action preparation — the concept behind WRH's "freeze is not a choice / refusal says I will not, freeze says I cannot" line. It does NOT support: the specific label "Dorsal Vagal Shutdown" (term absent), the session's universalized "your body *couldn't* do anything else" applied to every participant episode, the whiteboard chain in ER-396 (freeze → dissociation engages as mechanism), or any WRH participant-outcome claim. No part of the review evaluates WRH itself.

### S-T6-2 — Kozlowska et al. (2015): defense cascade; freezing vs. tonic/collapsed immobility; dissociation overlap

- **Full citation:** Kozlowska, K., Walker, P., McLean, L., & Carrive, P. (2015). Fear and the defense cascade: Clinical implications and management. *Harvard Review of Psychiatry, 23*(4), 263–287. Publication date: July 2015 (2015 Jul 8 per PMC). DOI: 10.1097/HRP.0000000000000065. Stable full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4495877/ (PMCID PMC4495877).
- **Inspection depth:** FULL TEXT — abstract, introduction, Figures 1–2, "Freezing: animal model" and "The freeze response in humans," "Tonic immobility: animal model" and "in humans," "Collapsed immobility" sections, and targeted `find` for "dissociat", "Porges", "dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus". Supplemental Text Boxes were not separately retrieved.
- **Design/population:** Integrative clinical review: PubMed searches plus clinical vignettes (children/adolescents/adults seen by the authors); NOT an experimental study.
- **Findings relevant to T6:**
  - Freezing is "a flight-or-fight response put on hold": VLPAG-imposed immobility with preserved muscle tone, co-activation of sympathetic and parasympathetic branches, opioid-mediated analgesia; in humans "a transient state… typically a short-lived phenomenon (often lasting only a few seconds), accessible for conscious processing."
  - **Freezing is distinguished from tonic immobility** (later in the cascade, after physical contact or when active defense failed; "terminal defense… shut-down response" with parasympathetic surge + sympathetic withdrawal) and from **collapsed immobility** (loss of muscle tone, cerebral hypoxia, compromised consciousness).
  - On dissociation vs. freeze/immobility: victims in tonic immobility report "dissociation (derealization and depersonalization)"; because of this overlap tonic immobility "has sometimes been conceptualized as a subtype of dissociation," and the authors note "a review of available data suggests that tonic immobility, peritraumatic dissociation, and dissociative PTSD may be mediated by a shared neural network." They also explicitly flag the assessment difficulty: "A key difficulty… is the dearth of studies that utilize standardized questions about the individual's subjective experience of immobility or that directly assess motor and autonomic state."
- **Limitations:** Author-stated: "dearth of studies of tonic immobility in humans"; much of the cascade neural-pattern account is animal-to-human translation plus clinical vignettes, not controlled experiments. The autonomic mediation (dorsal motor nucleus / unmyelinated vagal pathway framing) is explicitly tied to "Porges's polyvagal theory" — a framework that Phase 2 already records as substantially criticized (S11, Grossman et al. 2026 commentary) and not independently validated by its author exposition (S12). Nothing evaluates WRH or tests WRH's exact ER-396 ladder.
- **Applicability to WRH's exact wording:** Consistent with a GENERAL concept that freezing is involuntary and distinct from voluntary refusal, and that dissociation-related experiences (derealization/depersonalization) can co-occur with later-cascade immobility. It does NOT support WRH's deterministic ER-396 chain (freeze → "Dissociation engages" as a universal loop), the claim "Dissociation is not pathology" as a universal statement, or the "Dorsal Vagal Shutdown" label as settled mechanism for participant education. The review's own account shows the freeze/dissociation relationship is overlapping and under-specified, not a clean two-state teaching distinction.

## 4. Verdict

### 4a. What the inspected evidence establishes

1. **Freeze can be an involuntary, defensive state — but as a laboratory-observed phenomenon, not as a clinical certainty.** Roelofs (2017, full text) establishes that freezing is a real, parasympathetically dominated, action-preparatory defense state in animal work and in human lab paradigms (heart-rate deceleration + reduced body sway). This supports the GENERAL concept behind WRH's freeze-vs-noncompliance distinction (handout-13: "Freeze is not a choice… Refusal says 'I will not.' Freeze says 'I cannot.'").
2. **Freezing and later-cascade immobility are distinct phenomena in the literature.** Both Roelofs and Kozlowska distinguish freezing (alert, action-preparatory, early) from tonic immobility (later, after contact or failed active defense) and collapsed immobility (with loss of muscle tone and compromised consciousness). This supports treating "freeze" as a specific construct rather than a catch-all — a point WRH's education partially encodes by separating Sessions 09/13 (freeze) from Sessions 10/14/41 (dissociation).
3. **The freeze↔dissociation relationship is scientifically unsettled, and the unsettled direction cuts against WRH's certainty.** Kozlowska notes dissociation-like experiences can co-occur with tonic immobility and a hypothesized shared network; Danböck (2024) found NO link between acute dissociation and bodily/facial immobility markers; Beutler (2022) found NO robust evidence for a distinct hypoarousal ANS fingerprint of dissociation. WRH's ER-396 ladder ("Freeze isn't enough protection → Dissociation engages") and the "Dorsal Vagal Shutdown" label go beyond what these sources support as settled.
4. **Diagnostic overshadowing is a real, documented phenomenon with documented harms** (Hallyburton 2022: misattribution → worsening conditions, decreased care-seeking, death; record "flagging" primes later assessors). This gives the backlog's two-sided risk statement an empirical analogue: an authoritative label — including a NEW label like "System-Mismatched" — can itself become the frame through which subsequent symptoms are interpreted.
5. **Self-stigma can be reduced by stigma-focused group programs in at least one pilot population** (Drapalski et al., ESS-P, N = 57 veterans with PTSD: self-stigma d = −0.77). This supports the general plausibility of the shame-relief side of WRH's reframe — but for ESS-P's protocol and population only.

### 4b. What the inspected evidence does NOT establish

1. **None of WRH's exact diagnostic claims.** No inspected source tests or supports: "System-Mismatched" as an identity label; "You are reacting to the system mismatch, not just your own symptoms" as a diagnostic attribution to participants; any diagnosis-equivalence translation ('Bipolar' = 'Sympathetic/Dorsal Vagal Oscillations', etc.); "Dissociation is not pathology" as a universal statement; or "This isn't 'anxiety.' It is physiological data." Support for the general involuntariness of freezing does not support these specific wordings or their delivery to participants.
2. **The "Dorsal Vagal Shutdown" mechanism as presented.** The exact phrase does not appear in Roelofs's full text; Kozlowska's DMN/vagal account is explicitly framed through Porges's polyvagal theory, which Phase 2 records as substantially criticized (S11) and not independently validated (S12). A reviewer must decide whether this vocabulary is appropriate for participant education.
3. **The ER-396 deterministic chain** (trigger → freeze → dissociation engages → "No memory, no action, no connection"). Danböck's null behavioral finding and Beutler's null ANS finding both point away from teaching this ladder as established mechanism.
4. **That the reframes are safe or effective.** Nothing inspected evaluates WRH's actual materials, participant responses to them, or whether they relieve shame vs. redirect assessment. ESS-P's pilot result is about a different intervention; Hallyburton's harms are about a different direction of misclassification in clinical care.

### 4c. Unresolved

1. **Freeze vs. dissociation boundaries** remain contested: overlap in subjective experience (Kozlowska), failed behavioral-link test (Danböck), no consistent physiological fingerprint (Beutler). Whether WRH's two-track treatment (freeze sessions vs. dissociation sessions) matches the best-supported clinical framing is a judgment call for qualified review, not for this worker file.
2. **The exact direction of misclassification risk for WRH's reframes:** plausible mechanisms exist for both shame relief (ESS-P analogue) and redirected assessment (overshadowing analogue), but neither has been tested for WRH's specific materials. This is the precise question the backlog posed, and it remains unanswered by inspection.
3. **Whether the diagnosis-translation equivalents (Sessions 59–66) constitute diagnostic guidance** that could influence participants' or facilitators' treatment decisions — no inspected source addresses participant-facing relabeling of DSM diagnoses.

### 4d. Precise questions for qualified clinical review

1. Which diagnostic-tagged claims, if any, may be delivered to participants as stated facts, versus restricted to facilitator background, versus removed entirely? (Applies to the full 109-entry list; at minimum the entries in §2.)
2. Should the "System-Mismatched" identity label (ER-685 family, 7 entries) be retired as a potential reverse-overshadowing frame — a new label that participants adopt as what they ARE, with the same care-impeding properties Hallyburton attributes to flagging?
3. Are the diagnosis-equivalence translations (ER-567–569 and the 12 further '=' entries) clinically safe in participant hands, or do they risk misdiagnosis-relevant misunderstandings; must they carry an explicit "this does not change or replace any clinical diagnosis" boundary?
4. Is "Dorsal Vagal Shutdown" (ER-126 and Session 13/41 framing) defensible participant-facing mechanistic language given that Roelofs does not use the term and the DMN/vagal account rests on a contested framework (Phase 2 S11/S12)?
5. Does the universal claim "Dissociation is not pathology… It kept you alive" (ER-132) risk normalizing pathological dissociation that requires clinical assessment; under what audience and qualification conditions could any adaptive-framing of dissociation be safe?
6. Can the ER-396 trigger→freeze→dissociation ladder be taught as a mechanism given Danböck (2024) and Beutler (2022), or must it be downgraded or removed?
7. What guardrails must accompany any retained reframe to manage the two-sided misclassification risk — e.g., required statements that a reframe does not replace medical or psychiatric assessment, facilitator scripts for red-flag symptoms, and documentation practices?
8. What evidence would be required to claim any of these reframes reduces shame — is an ESS-P-style pilot (population, measures, design) the appropriate bar, and who owns that evidentiary burden?

### S-T7-1 — Milad & Quirk 2012 (fear extinction; prefrontal involvement)

- **Full citation:** Milad, M. R., & Quirk, G. J. (2012). Fear extinction as a model for translational neuroscience: Ten years of progress. *Annual Review of Psychology*, 63, 129–151.
- **Date:** 2012 (volume publication date January 2012).
- **DOI / stable URL:** DOI 10.1146/annurev.psych.121208.131631; PMID 22129456; PMCID PMC4942586 (open-access author manuscript at PMC).
- **Inspection depth:** Full text of the PMC open-access version — **selected sections inspected**: Abstract; "Overview of fear extinction and its psychological basis"; "Initial research on fear extinction mechanisms"; "The inhibitor found? — Rodent infralimbic prefrontal cortex"; Figure 2 caption. The full review was not read end-to-end.
- **Design / type:** Narrative translational review of rodent and human fear-conditioning/extinction research; not a primary study and not a study of WRH or of any named WRH audience.
- **Findings as inspected:**
  - Extinction is active new learning, not unlearning: "spontaneous recovery, fear renewal, reinstatement, as well as accelerated re-acquisition … constitute strong evidence that extinction does not erase the initial association between the CS and US but forms a new association (CS–No US) that inhibits expression of the conditioned memory." Extinction also "required NMDA receptors in the BLA, confirming that extinction was an active form of learning similar to conditioning itself."
  - Extinction memory expression is context-gated: an animal conditioned in context A and extinguished in context B expresses the extinction memory only when the cue is presented in context B; passage of time can itself act as a context shift.
  - Prefrontal cortex has a specific, retrievable role: rodent infralimbic (IL) lesions did not impair within-session extinction learning but impaired *retrieval* of extinction the following day; IL neurons signalled tones during retrieval, with response magnitude inversely correlated with freezing; mimicking IL tone responses with microstimulation reduced fear and strengthened extinction. The review's Figure 2 caption states the rodent IL and human ventromedial prefrontal cortex "inhibit fear expression and promote extinction."
  - The review also records the clinical framing that PTSD had been hypothesised to involve strong associations "resistant to extinction," and that PET/fMRI studies reported decreased prefrontal blood flow / reduced vmPFC activation in PTSD patients — i.e., reduced prefrontal engagement in a clinical group, described as a group-level imaging finding, not as a universal "never."
- **Limitations:** Review of (mostly laboratory) conditioning paradigms; animal-to-human translation is the review's stated project, not a completed proof for any individual; extinction effects are explicitly fragile and context-dependent in this literature, so the same source that shows change is possible also shows change is not guaranteed or permanent.
- **Applicability to WRH's exact wording:** Supports the *general concept* that threat responding is modifiable by new learning and that prefrontal systems participate actively in that modification (inconsistent with a literal universal "prefrontal cortex never even gets a vote" and with literal irreversibility). Does **not** support: "biological reality/cost" as a label for any specific WRH claim; any claim about self-care permission in "original wiring"; any WRH procedure; any promised WRH outcome. Equally, it does not support ER-524's opposite absolute (a recovered voice "doesn't go quiet again"), because the extinction literature it reviews documents return of extinguished responding (renewal, spontaneous recovery, reinstatement).

### S-T7-2 — Trask, Schepers & Bouton 2016 (extinction as new, context-bound learning; relapse phenomena)

- **Full citation:** Trask, S., Schepers, S. T., & Bouton, M. E. (2016). Context change explains resurgence after the extinction of operant behavior. *Journal of the Experimental Analysis of Behavior* (PMC author manuscript). PMID 27429503; PMCID PMC4943327.
- **Date:** 2016.
- **Stable URL:** PMCID PMC4943327 (PMC). No DOI was recorded from the inspected page; none is asserted here.
- **Inspection depth:** Full text of the PMC version — **selected sections inspected**: Abstract; introduction/renewal discussion; "Basic Features of Resurgence" (partial).
- **Design / type:** Review/theoretical paper on operant extinction and relapse phenomena, primarily nonhuman-animal laboratory research, with a section arguing relevance to human contingency-management and functional-communication-training relapse.
- **Findings as inspected:**
  - "The suppression of operant responding that occurs when reinforcers are omitted should not be taken as evidence that the original learning has been erased or unlearned."
  - Renewal: behaviour acquired in context A and extinguished in context B recovers when tested back in A (ABA), in a novel context C (ABC), or when acquired/extinguished in A and tested in B (AAB); "simple removal from the context of extinction is sufficient to cause responding to recover."
  - "Extinction results in new learning that is especially dependent upon the context in which it is learned," including learning to inhibit a specific response in a specific context; resurgence (return of an extinguished response when a replacement response is itself extinguished) is presented as a further relapse phenomenon with the same implication.
- **Limitations:** Operant (instrumental) extinction in animals, not human fear/trauma treatment; the human-treatment relevance is the authors' argument, not human outcome data inspected here.
- **Applicability to WRH's exact wording:** Same double-edged bearing as S-T7-1: behaviour change via new learning is real in this literature (against literal permanence-of-damage absolutes), and that change is context-bound and relapse-prone (against literal permanence-of-recovery absolutes such as ER-524). It says nothing about WRH's specific claims, sessions, or participants.
- **Related lead, not relied on:** Bouton, M. E. (2004), "Context and behavioral processes in extinction," *Learning & Memory*, 11, 485–494 — the canonical statement of this account. The copy located in this search (ResearchGate) did not yield inspectable full text, so Bouton 2004 itself is recorded here as **not inspected**; only the Bouton-co-authored 2016 review above was inspected.

## 4. Verdict

### What the evidence inspected establishes

1. **Threat responding is modifiable by new learning, in animals and in humans, at the level of general concept.** Extinction is an active, protein-synthesis- and NMDA-dependent learning process that forms a new inhibitory association rather than erasing the old one (S-T7-1, S-T7-2). Prefrontal (vmPFC/infralimbic) systems have a specific causal role in retrieving extinction and inhibiting fear expression, including microstimulation evidence in rodents and a stated human homologue (S-T7-1).
2. **Treatment-associated brain change has been observed in PTSD at group level.** Zhu et al. 2018 (abstract only) reports connectivity changes after prolonged exposure in a small PTSD sample — a counterexample to assuming unchanging circuitry in every person, and nothing stronger (S-T7-3).
3. **Human outcomes after potentially traumatic events are heterogeneous, and recovery is a documented trajectory.** In the trajectory literature reviewed by Bonanno et al. (abstract inspected), the most common trajectory is stable healthy functioning (resilience), and a distinct recovery trajectory — elevated symptoms returning gradually to baseline functioning — is also documented, alongside chronic and delayed trajectories (S-T7-4).
4. **A current agency guideline treats PTSD as treatable.** The 2023 VA/DoD CPG (currency verified at overview level) recommends specific trauma-focused psychotherapies as first-line, effective treatments for PTSD (S-T7-5).

### What the evidence inspected does not establish

1. **It does not support any T7 entry's exact wording.** No inspected source states or implies that self-care was "never permitted in the original wiring" (ER-416), that exhaustion/forgetfulness in a given case "wasn't a choice; it was the biological cost" of a named system (ER-533 and the ER-624–765 template family), that truthful speech activates an identical survival system as a "biological reality" (ER-491), or that a threat dial is "permanently soldered to ON" (ER-013). The absolutes — "never," "permanently," "biological reality/cost" as fact-labels — are precisely the elements no inspected source carries.
2. **It does not support the prefrontal-exclusion absolute.** S-T7-1 documents reduced prefrontal activation in PTSD groups and a mechanistic prefrontal role in extinction retrieval; neither is a universal "never even gets a vote," and the retrieval role is evidence of prefrontal participation in change, not of its permanent exclusion (ER-463).
3. **It does not support the opposite absolute either.** ER-524's permanent-recovery claim ("doesn't go quiet again") is contradicted in form by the same literature: extinction gains are context-dependent and subject to renewal, spontaneous recovery, reinstatement, and resurgence (S-T7-1, S-T7-2), and chronic/delayed trajectories exist (S-T7-4).
4. **It does not validate WRH.** No inspected source evaluates WRH, its sessions, its protocols, its facilitator-led group setting, or its outcomes. General findings about extinction, plasticity, trajectories, or PTSD treatment must not be read as validation of WRH's wording, procedures, or promised effects.
5. **Group-level and animal findings do not settle any individual's case** — including the Architect case narrative the T7 closings characterise. Zhu et al.'s limits (S-T7-3) and ER-013's own field 4 both record this.
