# FACILITATOR GUIDE — CORE LESSONS 1–4

## Companion to Facilitator_Guide_Lessons_05_08.md

This guide covers Core Lessons 1–4 of the four-track (Lane D) sequence: What Is a Survival Response?; Safety vs. Familiarity; Fight, Flight, Freeze & Shutdown; and Impact vs. Intention. It mirrors the structure and conventions of the existing guide for Lessons 5–8. Presence in this repository is not approval. No lesson, workbook, or guide in this sequence is approved or clinically reviewed, and delivery remains withheld.

### How wording is marked in this guide

- **Participant-facing examples** appear in blockquotes and track tables. A **Say:** label marks an example spoken prompt; other quotations include scenario dialogue, teaching anchors, and example answers. These are draft teaching examples, not delivery authorization. Unquoted notes are for facilitators only.
- **Facilitator notes** — everything outside the blockquotes — are preparation and delivery guidance for the facilitator only. Do not read facilitator notes aloud. Research and review notes appear only in facilitator notes, never in participant-facing wording.

### Facilitator Operating Standard

The facilitator is not there to prove a participant wrong.

The job is to help the participant move from **automatic interpretation → accurate observation → personal responsibility → practical action**.

Across all four tracks, keep the same governing standard:

> **Understand the behavior. Tell the truth about the behavior. Own the behavior. Change what can be changed.**

No forced disclosure. No diagnostic language. No assumption that the participant's perception is automatically wrong. No assumption that the institution, other person, or participant is automatically right.

The lessons follow the curriculum sequence:

> **Show it → Name it → Explain it → Separate it → Interrupt it → Practice it → Carry it.**

Each lesson is taught in the setting track that matches the group: **Corrections, Reentry, Veteran, or Workplace**. The core lesson does not change by track; the scenario, examples, and carry-forward lines do. Keep the tracks distinct: do not deliver another track's scenario as if it were this group's, and do not mix track wording inside one session.

### Participation standard (all four lessons)

Proposed WRH learning choices for all four lessons (subject to site and accessibility review):

1. Speak in the group.
2. Write privately in the workbook without sharing it.
3. Work with the provided scenario or a fictional situation instead of a personal one.
4. Listen or observe without speaking or completing written work. A participant may pass on any exercise without explaining why.

No WRH exercise requires personal disclosure or an explanation for passing. These are proposed learning choices, not a claim about institutional attendance, records, or accommodation policies. Those policies require separate review. State the choices when introducing each lesson.

### Self-assessment standard (all four lessons)

Each lesson ends with a Recognize / Own / Change self-rating (0–2). Facilitator note: these ratings are **reflection tools for the participant's own use**. They are not measures of clinical progress, treatment effect, or lesson effectiveness, and facilitators do not collect, score, or report them as outcomes. The tables in this guide describe what each score looks like as self-observation, matching the workbook's scale.

> **⚠ QUALIFIED-REVIEW DEPENDENCY — READ BEFORE ANY USE OF THIS GUIDE**
>
> This guide does **not** contain clinical screening, crisis-response, or escalation procedures, and none are authorized by it. # CORE LESSON 1 — WHAT IS A SURVIVAL RESPONSE?

## 1. Objectives

Draft learning objectives — participants are invited to practice:

- Describe, in plain words, that the body can react to a perceived threat before all the facts are known.
- Name at least one of their own early signals (body, mind, or behavior) from the workbook checklist.
- Separate four things: perceived threat from confirmed threat, automatic response from chosen behavior, explanation from excuse, and another person's action from their own response.
- Use the interrupt question for their track and produce one neutral opening response to the practice scenario.
- State the lesson's core distinction: a survival response can explain a reaction without excusing the behavior that follows.

## 2. Preparation

Facilitator prepares before the session:

- Confirm which setting track (Corrections, Reentry, Veteran, Workplace) this group uses, and teach only that track's scenario and lines from Lesson 1.
- Read Lesson 1 in full, all four tracks, so track wording is not accidentally mixed.
- Have Workbook Lesson 1 available for every participant, plus a private writing option (paper) for anyone who does not want to write in the workbook.
- Write the core distinction and the track's interrupt question on the board before the group arrives.
- Review the participation standard above; be ready to state it in the first two minutes.

Facilitator note — breathing option in the workbook: Workbook Lesson 1, Interrupt section, offers an optional pause with slow breathing as one checkbox among several. Present it exactly as the workbook does — optional, uncounted, and skippable — and do not present any breathing pattern as a validated technique or a required step. The Phase 4 dossier records research on several breathing practices and their different conditions. It does not validate this WRH workbook option or establish its suitability for any participant or setting. If a participant says breathing focus makes them feel worse, the workbook's other interrupt options (ask what is actually happening, ask for clarification, take appropriate space, listen before defending) are complete alternatives, not lesser ones.

Facilitator note — naming: the lessons teach naming as putting the reaction into words ("My body is reacting as if something bad is about to happen"). Do not add a mechanism claim for naming — no timing, brain, or "air" claim is part of this lesson, and none is supported for this use. Naming here is a description step, nothing more.

## 3. Facilitator Anchor

### Core Trap

The central trap is:

> **“Because my body reacted, the situation must be what my body says it is — and what I did next was just the reaction.”**

Common versions:

* “I knew something was wrong. My body doesn't lie.”
* “I couldn't help it. That's just how I react.”
* “They started it by making me feel that way.”
* “If I feel threatened, it is a threat.”

A related trap is:

> **“A real reaction means I have no responsibility for anything that followed.”**

Neither is the standard.

### Facilitator Reframe

> **“The reaction is information. What you do after you notice it is the part you can work on.”**

The facilitator should not argue with the reaction itself.

Instead, add the fact-check:

> **“Notice the response. Get the facts. Choose the next move.”**

### Anchor Line

> **“Recognize the response before letting it run the interaction.”**

### Common Pushback

**Participant:**

> “You weren't there. My body knew.”

**Facilitator:**

> **Say:** “Your body reacted — that part is not in dispute. The lesson asks one more question: what did you actually know at that moment, and what did you find out afterward?”

That response accepts the reaction without accepting the conclusion the reaction produced.

## 4. Teaching Sequence

Teach in this order. Times are a guide for a single session; do not cut the participation statement or the practice to save time.

1. **Open (5 minutes).** State the participation standard. State the lesson title and the core distinction.
   > **Say:** “A survival response can explain a reaction without excusing the behavior that follows.”
2. **Core connection (5 minutes).** Teach the track's Core Connection: a person can react to perceived threat before fully processing what is happening; the body may tense, attention may narrow, and the person may become angry, defensive, silent, or ready to leave.
3. **Scenario (5 minutes).** Read the track's Recognizable Scenario aloud (see the track table in §5). Ask only: what happened first, and what did the person assume?
4. **Seven-Step Application (20 minutes).** Work the track's steps in order — Recognize, Name, Explain, Separate, Interrupt, Practice, Carry It — using the scenario, not a participant's personal history. Participants complete Workbook Lesson 1 §§4–8 alongside each step.
5. **Accountability and Boundary Checks (5 minutes).** Teach both questions exactly as the lesson states them: “What part belongs to me?” and “What part does not belong to me?” Keep both. Use both questions; do not ask participants to take responsibility for other people's actions.
6. **Next Action and Carry-Forward (5 minutes).** Each participant completes Workbook §12 (one specific behavior) and hears the track's Carry-Forward line (§5 table) and the workbook's shared Carry-It Statement: “Notice the response. Get the facts. Choose the next move.”

## 5. Track Distinctions — Lesson 1

Keep these distinct. Teach one row only: the group's track.

| Track | Scenario (summary) | Interrupt question (participant-facing) | Practice response (participant-facing) | Carry-Forward (participant-facing) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Corrections | Staff member says “I need to talk with you”; participant tenses, assumes a consequence is coming, becomes argumentative before hearing why. | “What do I actually know right now?” | “Okay. What is this about?” | “Notice the response. Get the facts. Choose the next move.” |
| Reentry | Email says “We need additional information before we can process your application”; person assumes rejection, becomes angry, stops responding. The message did not say rejected. | “What actually happened?” then “What do I know, and what am I predicting?” | Respond to the actual message: provide the information; ask what is needed. | “Respond to what happened—not only to what you fear will happen.” |
| Veteran | Crowded workplace meeting; someone moves quickly behind the participant, who turns, tenses, and responds sharply. The other person was trying to get past. | “What is actually happening right now?” | Pause, look at the environment, identify what is actually occurring, then respond to the current situation. | “Use awareness to gather information, not automatically to declare danger.” |
| Workplace | Supervisor says “Can we talk about yesterday's work?”; employee assumes they are being fired, tightens, becomes defensive. Supervisor wants to discuss one correctable mistake. | “What did the person actually say?” | “Sure. What would you like to go over?” | “Hear the whole message before deciding what it means.” |

Track-specific teaching points (facilitator notes):

- **Corrections:** The scenario turns on authority and consequences. Do not ask participants to discuss real disciplinary situations; stay with the scenario as written.
- **Reentry:** The teaching point is the gap between the message received and the outcome predicted. Keep attention on that gap, not on the institution's decision.
- **Veteran:** The lesson's wording is that a rapid response “may have been useful in one environment and less useful in another.” Keep that wording. Do not reframe any participant's service history.
- **Workplace:** Separate feedback from attack, and discomfort from danger, using the lesson's Separate list verbatim before inviting examples.

## 6. Practice — Five-Minute Practicum

Use the workbook scenario (Workbook Lesson 1, §9), which is the shared cross-track scenario for this lesson.

### Scenario

Someone approaches you and says:

> **“We need to talk.”**

You immediately assume something bad is about to happen. Your body reacts. Your first impulse is to become defensive.

### Minute 1 — Automatic Reaction

Participant writes (Workbook §9, Step 1):

> **What is my automatic reaction?**

### Minute 2 — Facts

Participant writes (Step 2):

> **What do I actually know?**

Expected content: the person said “We need to talk,” and the scenario has not supplied a reason yet.

### Minute 3 — Assumption

Participant writes (Step 3):

> **What am I assuming?**

### Minute 4 — Different Response

Participant writes (Step 4) one thing they could do differently, then reads or writes the practice response:

> **Say (model):** “Okay. What do you need to talk about?”

Participants then write their own version. Sharing their version aloud is optional; writing it is the practice.

### Minute 5 — Carry It

Participant states or writes:

> **“I noticed the reaction, separated fact from assumption, and chose what happened next.”**

Facilitator note: the goal line in the workbook is explicit — the goal is **not** “I should not have reacted.” Do not let the practicum turn into criticism of the first reaction. The practiced skill is the step after it.

## 7. Discussion Prompts

Use the provided scenario first. Personal examples are always optional and may stay general (“a time at work,” with no names or details).

1. “In the scenario, what did the body decide before the person had any facts?”
2. “What is the difference between ‘my body reacted’ and ‘the situation was dangerous’?”
3. “Which is harder to separate in practice: perceived threat from confirmed threat, or explanation from excuse? Why?”
4. “What does the interrupt question do that arguing with the feeling does not?”
5. “Name one early signal — body, mind, or behavior — that could work as a warning sign. You can take it from the workbook checklist instead of from your own life.”

## 8. Participation Choices

Proposed learning choices for this lesson (see the front-matter site/accessibility boundary):

- Answer a discussion prompt aloud, using the scenario.
- Complete Workbook §§4–12 in writing and share nothing aloud.
- Use a fictional situation in the Optional Reflection (§15) instead of a personal one.
- Name a workbook checklist signal instead of describing a personal experience.
- Pass on any prompt without explanation; the facilitator moves on without comment.

Facilitator note: a participant's silence in discussion is not evidence about their response pattern, and this lesson must not be used to interpret it. Lesson 3 covers shutdown; do not import that discussion here.

## 9. Room Dynamics

### Pushback: “This is just telling us our reactions are wrong.”

> **Say:** “No. The reaction is treated as information in this lesson. What we examine is the step after it — what you did with it.”

---

### Minimization: “Everyone reacts like that. It's normal, so it doesn't matter.”

> **Say:** “It can be common and still create a problem in a specific interaction. Let's stay with the scenario: what did the reaction cost the person in it?”

---

### Derailment: Participant starts arguing about whether a specific staff member, supervisor, or family member was right or wrong.

> **Say:** “We don't have to decide that person. In the scenario, what did this person know, what did they assume, and what did they do next?”

---

### Participant Says: “So my feelings are lying to me.”

> **Say:** “No. A feeling is real. The lesson separates the feeling from the conclusion: ‘I feel threatened’ and ‘this is a threat’ are two different statements.”

---

### Forced Disclosure

If someone presses another participant for a personal example:

> **Say:** “Nobody has to use a personal example in this lesson. The scenario and the checklist are enough to complete every exercise.”

---

### Distress during the lesson

> **⚠ QUALIFIED-REVIEW DEPENDENCY:** This lesson's material — threat, tension, strong reactions — can itself be activating. The front-matter dependency note supplies no acute-distress response procedure or authority. The clinical and site response remains undecided and requires qualified review before delivery. Do not present the workbook's optional breathing checkbox as a distress procedure.

## 10. Checks for Understanding

Ask these before the closing. A correct answer uses the scenario or the workbook's wording, not a personal disclosure. Offer to revisit unclear steps; do not require an answer or inspect private writing as a condition of passing.

1. **Definition:** “In your own words: what is a survival response in this lesson?” (Expected: the body reacting to a perceived threat before the facts are known.)
2. **Distinction:** “A response explains a reaction. What does it not do?” (Expected: it does not excuse or determine the behavior that follows.)
3. **Separation:** “In the scenario, name one thing that was known and one thing that was assumed.”
4. **Interrupt:** “What is this track's interrupt question, and when would you ask it?” (Use the §5 table for the group's track.)
5. **Application:** “Your workbook Next Action (§12) names one specific behavior. Read yours back to yourself: is it specific enough that you would know whether you did it?”

## 11. Scoring & Self-Assessment

Workbook Lesson 1, §14. Facilitator reminder: reflection tool only — see the self-assessment standard in the front matter.

| Score | Recognize | Own | Change |
| ----- | --------- | --- | ------ |
| **0** | I did not notice my response. | I placed the whole interaction on the other person or the situation. | I cannot yet name a different next action. |
| **1** | I noticed my response afterward. | I can identify my part after the fact. | I can identify a different next action I could have taken. |
| **2** | I can notice my response as it begins. | I can identify my part in the moment. | I can practice the different next action I identified. |

## 12. Closing

1. Read the core distinction once more.
   > **Say:** “A survival response can explain a reaction without excusing the behavior that follows.”
2. Read the group's track Carry-Forward line (§5 table) and then the workbook's shared Carry-It Statement:
   > **Say:** “Notice the response. Get the facts. Choose the next move.”
3. Remind the group of their written Next Action (Workbook §12) — one specific behavior, not a general intention.
4. State the Optional Reflection (§15) is optional and may use a fictional situation.
5. Facilitator note: do not close with a promise about what recognizing the response will do for participants. The lesson's own claim is narrower: recognizing creates a chance to interrupt the response and choose the next move. Keep the closing inside that claim.

# CORE LESSON 2 — SAFETY VS. FAMILIARITY

## 1. Objectives

Draft learning objectives — participants are invited to practice:

- State the lesson's two-sided distinction in both directions: unfamiliar does not automatically mean unsafe, and familiar does not automatically mean safe.
- Identify one way discomfort, suspicion, or an urge to reject can appear when something is unfamiliar (Workbook Lesson 2, §§4–5).
- Separate unfamiliar from dangerous, cautious from closed, and reasonable evidence from automatic suspicion, using their track's Separate list.
- Use their track's interrupt question to name what evidence they actually have before deciding.
- Name one fact they would check before accepting or rejecting an unfamiliar situation, while keeping a reasonable boundary.

## 2. Preparation

- Confirm the group's track and teach only that track's scenario and lines from Lesson 2.
- Read Lesson 2 in full, all four tracks. Teach both halves of the shared workbook distinction in every track — do not teach only “unfamiliar is not unsafe.” The track files vary in how explicitly they state each half.
- Have Workbook Lesson 2 available for every participant, plus the private writing option.
- Write both halves of the core distinction on the board before the group arrives:
  > **“Unfamiliar does not automatically mean unsafe.”**
  > **“Familiar does not automatically mean safe.”**
- Facilitator note — what this lesson is not: this lesson teaches checking evidence before deciding. It does **not** teach participants to override evidence of actual danger, to trust everyone, or to stay in a situation that is actually unsafe. Every track's Boundary Check says so explicitly (for example, Reentry: “I do not have to stay in a situation that is actually unsafe or disrespectful”). If the teaching drifts toward “always give it a chance,” it has left the lesson.

## 3. Facilitator Anchor

### Core Trap

The central trap is:

> **“Because it feels familiar, it is safe — and because it feels unfamiliar, it is dangerous.”**

Common versions:

* “I know how this place works. That's safer.”
* “There has to be a catch.”
* “I can't live like this” — said in the first hour of a stable arrangement.
* “People who offer help are usually setting you up.”

The opposite trap is equally important:

> **“If I'm told to question my suspicion, I'm being told to trust blindly and drop my boundaries.”**

Neither is the standard. The lesson explicitly does not teach blind acceptance in any track.

### Facilitator Reframe

> **“A feeling is information. It is not automatically evidence.”**

The facilitator should not tell participants their caution is wrong.

Instead, add the evidence check:

> **“What evidence tells me this is unsafe — and what evidence am I actually using?”**

(That is the Corrections track's interrupt question; each track's own question is in the §5 table.)

### Anchor Line

> **“Learn to separate what is familiar from what is actually safe.”**

### Common Pushback

**Participant:**

> “Every time I trusted something new, it went bad.”

**Facilitator:**

> **Say:** “You are describing past situations. The lesson doesn't ask you to ignore them. It asks what evidence you have about *this* situation, separate from the ones before it.”

That response keeps the participant's experience in the room without letting it decide the current case by itself.

## 4. Teaching Sequence

1. **Open (5 minutes).** State the participation standard. State the title and both halves of the core distinction.
2. **Core connection (5 minutes).** Teach the track's Core Connection: safety and familiarity are not the same thing; a person can become comfortable with a harmful or limiting situation because it is familiar, and a safer situation can feel wrong because it is unfamiliar.
3. **Scenario (5 minutes).** Read the track's Recognizable Scenario aloud (§5 table). Ask only: what did the person reject or resist, and what evidence did they have at that moment?
4. **Seven-Step Application (20 minutes).** Work the track's steps in order — Recognize, Name, Explain, Separate, Interrupt, Practice, Carry It — using the scenario. Participants complete Workbook Lesson 2 §§4–8 alongside. In Practice, keep the lesson's own standard: gather information, ask questions, look at the facts, **then** decide — in either direction.
5. **Accountability and Boundary Checks (5 minutes).** Teach both questions. The Boundary Check carries real weight in this lesson: participants do not have to trust everyone, accept every offer, or stay in an actually unsafe situation. Say so plainly.
6. **Next Action and Carry-Forward (5 minutes).** Workbook §12 (check one thing before deciding) and the track's Carry-Forward line (§5 table), then the workbook's shared Carry-It Statement: “I can stay cautious without treating unfamiliarity as proof of danger.”

## 5. Track Distinctions — Lesson 2

| Track | Scenario (summary) | Interrupt question (participant-facing) | Practice (participant-facing) | Carry-Forward (participant-facing) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Corrections | Participant is offered a legitimate opportunity to work with a program or staff member; assumes “There has to be a catch” and rejects it because distrust feels safer. | “What evidence tells me this is unsafe?” then “What evidence am I actually using?” | Gather information before accepting or rejecting: ask questions, look at the facts, then decide. | “Check the facts before letting familiarity make the decision.” |
| Reentry | Person gets a chance to live somewhere stable with reasonable rules; thinks “I can't live like this,” leaves, and returns to a familiar situation that is making life harder. | “Am I rejecting this because it is unsafe, or because I am not used to it?” | Give the new situation enough time to gather evidence: follow the rules, ask questions, notice what actually happens, then decide. | “Do not confuse unfamiliar with unsafe.” |
| Veteran | Civilian workplace has unclear instructions; participant becomes frustrated, assumes “These people have no idea what they are doing,” and disengages. | “Is there a real problem here, or is this simply not how I am used to doing things?” | Identify what actually needs clarification; ask directly; address genuine problems without treating every difference as a threat. | “Learn the new environment before judging it.” |
| Workplace | Supervisor says “Let me know if you need help”; employee assumes the offer is monitoring, refuses help, and struggles alone. | “What has this person actually done?” | Accept a reasonable offer of assistance without giving away judgment or boundaries; observe what happens. | “Stay cautious. Stay open to evidence.” |

Track-specific teaching points (facilitator notes):

- **Corrections:** The scenario is about an opportunity, not about compliance. Do not use this lesson to argue participants into accepting any specific program or person.
- **Reentry:** The familiar situation the person returns to “is making life harder” — the lesson's words. Do not soften that, and do not name or discuss any participant's actual housing situation.
- **Veteran:** The lesson allows that “the problem may be real.” Teach the Separate list (different from wrong; unfamiliar from unsafe; lack of structure from actual danger; preference from necessity) before any judgment about the scenario.
- **Workplace:** The Carry It line for this track is “I can stay cautious without automatically rejecting support.” Keep “cautious” in the sentence when you teach it; dropping it changes the lesson.

## 6. Practice — Five-Minute Practicum

Use the workbook scenario (Workbook Lesson 2, §9).

### Scenario

A new person enters your environment and offers to help you.

Your immediate thought is:

> **“I don't trust them.”**

### Minute 1 — Know

Participant writes (Workbook §9):

> **What do I know?**

### Minute 2 — Assume

Participant writes:

> **What am I assuming?**

### Minute 3 — Evidence Needed

Participant writes:

> **What evidence would I need?**

### Minute 4 — Boundary and Response

Participant writes one reasonable boundary they could keep while finding out, then reads or writes the practice response:

> **Say (model):** “I appreciate the offer. Let me think about it.”

Participants write their own version. Sharing aloud is optional.

### Minute 5 — Carry It

Participant states or writes the workbook skill line:

> **“Stay aware while gathering information.”**

Facilitator note: the practicum's correct outcome is **not** accepting the help. A participant who concludes, on evidence, that they will decline — with a boundary intact — has completed the skill. Say so if the room drifts toward treating acceptance as the right answer.

## 7. Discussion Prompts

1. “Give an example from the scenario work: something that was familiar but not safe, or unfamiliar but not dangerous. You can invent one.”
2. “What is the difference between being cautious and being closed? Use the lesson's Separate list.”
3. “Why can predictability feel easier than change, even when the predictable thing is harmful?” (Lesson wording, Reentry track Explain step.)
4. “What is one fact — not a feeling — that would help you decide about an unfamiliar offer?”
5. “What boundary can a person keep *while* they gather information, instead of deciding first?”

## 8. Participation Choices

Proposed learning choices for this lesson (see the front-matter site/accessibility boundary):

- Work the scenario and the workbook Interrupt questions (§8: know / assume / evidence for / evidence still needed / boundary) in writing, sharing nothing aloud.
- Answer a prompt using an invented example.
- Decline to discuss any real offer, placement, workplace, or relationship — the lesson's scenarios are sufficient for every exercise.
- Pass on any prompt without explanation.

Facilitator note: do not ask participants whether they personally rejected housing, help, employment, or a program. Those are the tracks' scenario contents, not discussion assignments.

## 9. Room Dynamics

### Pushback: “So we should just trust people now.”

> **Say:** “No. Read the Boundary Check with me: you do not have to trust everyone, accept every offer, or stay anywhere actually unsafe. The lesson is about deciding on evidence, in either direction.”

---

### Minimization: “It's just being careful. There's nothing to learn here.”

> **Say:** “Caution is kept in this lesson. The question is narrower: in the scenario, what did the person decide, and what evidence did the decision rest on?”

---

### Derailment: The group starts trading warnings about a specific person, program, or employer.

> **Say:** “Pause. We're deciding a real case now, and that's not this lesson's work. Back to the scenario: what was known, and what was assumed?”

---

### Participant Says: “My suspicion has kept me alive. You're telling me to drop it.”

> **Say:** “I'm not. The lesson's own line is ‘stay cautious.’ What it adds is one step: check what the suspicion rests on in *this* situation before it makes the whole decision.”

Facilitator note: do not debate the participant's history or claim the suspicion was unnecessary. The lesson makes no claim about any individual's past situation, and neither does the facilitator.

---

### Forced Disclosure

> **Say:** “You don't need to name a place you left or a person you distrust. Use the scenario, or describe the type of situation with no details.”

---

### Actual danger raised in the room

> **⚠ QUALIFIED-REVIEW DEPENDENCY:** If a participant describes a current situation they believe is actually dangerous, this lesson does not equip the facilitator to assess it, and this guide contains no assessment, safety-planning, or escalation procedure. Do not use the lesson's “unfamiliar ≠ unsafe” teaching on a participant's report of present danger. What the site requires in that moment is a clinical and site decision, flagged here for qualified review and **not** supplied by this document.

## 10. Checks for Understanding

1. **Distinction, both directions:** “State the core distinction without looking: what does ‘unfamiliar’ not automatically mean, and what does ‘familiar’ not automatically mean?”
2. **Evidence:** “In the practicum scenario, name one thing the person knew and one thing they only assumed.”
3. **Boundary:** “Name one boundary a person can keep while gathering information. Does keeping it require trusting the other person?”
4. **Interrupt:** “Say this track's interrupt question from memory, or find it in your workbook.” (Use the §5 table.)
5. **Application:** “Look at your Workbook §12. What is the one thing you wrote that you will check before deciding next time?”

## 11. Scoring & Self-Assessment

Workbook Lesson 2, §14. Facilitator reminder: reflection tool only — see the front-matter standard.

| Score | Recognize | Own | Change |
| ----- | --------- | --- | ------ |
| **0** | I treated unfamiliar as unsafe (or familiar as safe) without noticing. | I treated my interpretation as established fact. | I decided without gathering any information. |
| **1** | I noticed the unfamiliarity reaction afterward. | I can separate my interpretation from the facts afterward. | I can identify information I should have gathered. |
| **2** | I can notice when unfamiliarity is driving my reaction as it happens. | I can separate interpretation from fact in the moment. | I can gather information before reacting. |

## 12. Closing

1. Read both halves of the core distinction.
   > **Say:** “Unfamiliar does not automatically mean unsafe. Familiar does not automatically mean safe.”
2. Read the group's track Carry-Forward line (§5 table), then the workbook's shared Carry-It Statement:
   > **Say:** “I can stay cautious without treating unfamiliarity as proof of danger.”
3. Remind the group of their written Next Action (Workbook §12): check before deciding.
4. State the Optional Reflection (§15) is optional and does not require identifying anyone.
5. Facilitator note: close inside the lesson's claim. The lesson teaches a check — separate familiarity from safety and look at evidence — not an outcome. Do not promise that checking will make unfamiliar situations work out.

# CORE LESSON 3 — FIGHT, FLIGHT, FREEZE & SHUTDOWN

## 1. Objectives

Draft learning objectives — participants are invited to practice:

- Name the four response directions this lesson teaches — toward conflict (fight), away (flight), into stillness (freeze), and into shutdown — as patterns, in the Corrections track's words, “not permanent boxes or excuses.”
- Identify their most common pattern and its earliest recognizable sign (Workbook Lesson 3, §§4–5).
- Separate the first reaction from the next response: state what belongs to them after the initial reaction, including returning to the situation and communicating.
- Use one interruption line or smallest-next-step for their track (for example, “I need a minute, but I will answer,” or “What is the smallest thing I can handle first?”).
- State the core distinction: the first response is information; it does not have to become the final response.

## 2. Preparation

- Confirm the group's track and teach only that track's scenario and lines from Lesson 3. Title note: the lesson file's title is “Fight, Flight, Freeze & Shutdown”; the workbook now uses the same title form. Use that form on the board.
- Read Lesson 3 in full, all four tracks, and Workbook Lesson 3 in full.
- Write the four directions and the core distinction on the board before the group arrives.
- Facilitator note — freeze and refusal (read carefully): the Corrections scenario turns on a real distinction this lesson teaches behaviorally — in the scenario, silence under pressure is **not** the same act as a deliberate refusal to answer. Teach it at that level: as a distinction between two behaviors in a described situation, and as a reason to ask what happened before interpreting silence. Do **not** teach it as a diagnosis of any participant, do not label any participant's silence in the room as “freeze,” and do not use any clinical or nervous-system label for it — the lesson files use none, and this guide adds none. Phase 4 evidence review found general support for the concept that freezing can be involuntary; it did not establish a diagnostic test a facilitator can apply to a person in the room, and none is offered here.
- Facilitator note — “one second / a minute”: practice lines in this lesson use ordinary time words (“Give me a second,” “I need a minute”). They are requests for a brief pause, not measured effects. Do not present naming, pausing, or any line in this lesson as producing a timed brain or body effect; no such claim is in the lesson, and none is supported for this use.

## 3. Facilitator Anchor

### Core Trap

The central trap is:

> **“My first reaction is the whole story — it decides what happens next, and it also settles what kind of responder I am.”**

Common versions:

* “When I shut down, that's it. Nothing can be done.”
* “I froze, so I failed the whole situation.”
* “I always blow up. That's just my pattern.”
* “If I go quiet, everyone should just know why.”

The opposite trap is equally important:

> **“Because the first reaction was automatic, nothing after it belongs to me either.”**

Neither is the standard. The Corrections track states both halves: the patterns are “not permanent boxes or excuses.”

### Facilitator Reframe

> **“The first response is information. It does not have to become the final response.”**

The facilitator should not dispute that the first reaction happened or that it can be strong.

Instead, move to the next step:

> **“What is one small action in the opposite direction?”**

(That is the Corrections track's Interrupt step; each track's own interruption is in the §5 table.)

### Anchor Line

> **“I may not control the first reaction. I can work on the next response.”**

(Corrections track Carry It. The workbook's shared Carry-It Statement is: “I may not control the first reaction. I can practice the next one.”)

### Common Pushback

**Participant:**

> “Easy to say ‘just answer.’ When it happens, I *can't* talk.”

**Facilitator:**

> **Say:** “The lesson doesn't claim you can always talk in that moment — its scenario is exactly a person who did not know what to say. What it practices is the step the person *can* take: one sentence when they can give it, and returning to the situation afterward. Look at the Corrections Next Action: communicate one clear sentence instead of disappearing from the interaction.”

That response stays inside the lesson's actual claim — effort and return after the initial reaction — instead of promising control over the reaction itself.

## 4. Teaching Sequence

1. **Open (5 minutes).** State the participation standard. State the title and the core distinction.
2. **Core connection (5 minutes).** Teach the track's Core Connection: people respond to pressure in different directions — toward conflict, away, into stillness, or into shutdown — and the first reaction is information, not the final decision.
3. **Scenario (5 minutes).** Read the track's Recognizable Scenario aloud (§5 table). Ask only: which direction did the response move, and what happened as a result?
4. **Seven-Step Application (20 minutes).** Work the track's steps in order — Recognize, Name, Explain, Separate, Interrupt, Practice, Carry It — using the scenario. Participants complete Workbook Lesson 3 §§4–8 alongside. Keep the Explain step bounded by the lesson's own words: pressure “can narrow a person's ability to respond in the moment; that may explain the silence; it does not remove the need to communicate or address the situation.”
5. **Accountability and Boundary Checks (5 minutes).** Teach both questions. In this lesson the accountable part is consistently the *return*: communicating, following through, taking one concrete step. The boundary part is also real: participants do not control other people's interpretations, demands, or reactions.
6. **Next Action and Carry-Forward (5 minutes).** Workbook §12 (one concrete step, and how they will know they used their interruption strategy) and the track's Carry-Forward line (§5 table), then the workbook's shared Carry-It Statement: “I may not control the first reaction. I can practice the next one.”

## 5. Track Distinctions — Lesson 3

Track-specific teaching points (facilitator notes):

- **Corrections:** Hold both halves of the scenario at once: the silence was not a deliberate refusal in the scenario as written, **and** “the result is still a failure to communicate.” Do not let either half erase the other. Do not invite participants to relitigate real rule violations or real staff interpretations.

- **Veteran:** Teach the Separate list exactly: taking space from abandoning the conversation; calm from silence; privacy from avoidance; control from connection. The accountable step is the return, at the time stated.
- **Workplace:** The Separate list distinguishes not knowing from being incapable, and needing time from refusing responsibility. Keep embarrassment discussable only through the scenario; no participant has to describe a real meeting.

# CORE LESSON 4 — IMPACT VS. INTENTION

## 1. Objectives

Draft learning objectives — participants are invited to practice:

- State the core distinction: intention tells us what a person meant; impact tells us what happened; both can matter.
- Identify, from the scenario, all four elements the lesson separates: what was intended, what was actually done, how the other person experienced it, and what consequence followed.
- Name their own first response when told their behavior affected someone negatively (Workbook Lesson 4, §5), without being required to share it.
- Use their track's interrupt question and practice a replacement delivery that keeps the legitimate goal (space, avoiding an argument, solving a problem, staying focused) while changing the behavior.
- State what belongs to them (words, tone, posture, behavior, willingness to adjust or correct) and what does not (another person's interpretation, feelings, or choices).

## 2. Preparation

- Confirm the group's track and teach only that track's scenario and lines from Lesson 4.
- Read Lesson 4 in full, all four tracks, and Workbook Lesson 4 in full.
- Write the core distinction on the board before the group arrives:
  > **“Intention tells us what I meant. Impact tells us what happened. Both can matter.”**
- Facilitator note — hold both guardrails in this lesson at all times. The workbook states them in the same section: taking responsibility for impact “does not mean accepting every accusation made against you,” and “someone experiencing a negative impact does not automatically prove that you intended harm.” A session that teaches only “own the impact” or only “your intention is what counts” has left the lesson in either direction.
- Facilitator note — wording: the workbook's Remember line for this lesson reads “You can have a good intention and still need to correct behavior that created a problem.” Use “created a problem,” not “harmful behavior,” when you summarize: the lesson teaches participants to examine what actually happened before characterizing it, and pre-labeling the behavior settles the question the lesson is asking them to examine.

## 3. Facilitator Anchor

### Core Trap

The central trap is:

> **“Because I didn't intend it, the impact doesn't count — my intention is the whole account.”**

Common versions:

* “I wasn't trying to threaten anybody.”
* “I was just trying to help.”
* “I wasn't being rude.”
* “They misunderstood me, so there's nothing to look at.”

The opposite trap is equally important:

> **“Because someone felt a negative impact, my intention is a lie and I must accept their whole account of me.”**

Neither is the standard. The Workplace track states the resolution directly: a participant does not have to choose between defending their intention and correcting their impact.

### Facilitator Reframe

> **“Your intention explains your behavior. It does not erase its impact.”**

(Corrections track Carry It.)

The facilitator should not ask participants to surrender an intention that may be true.

Instead, add the second question:

> **“What did I mean, what actually happened, and what can I change?”**

### Anchor Line

> **“Keep the purpose. Adjust the delivery.”**

(Veteran track Carry It.)

### Common Pushback

**Participant:**

> “So now I have to apologize for things I didn't even do wrong.”

**Facilitator:**

> **Say:** “No. Look at the workbook's Separate table for this lesson: controlling another person's feelings and being responsible for everything someone feels are in the ‘does not belong to me’ column. What belongs to you is what you said and did, and how you respond to feedback. The lesson asks you to examine those accurately — including concluding, on the facts, that your delivery was fine.”

That response is important because the lesson permits an accurate defense; it asks only that the defense come after examining the behavior, not instead of it.

## 4. Teaching Sequence

1. **Open (5 minutes).** State the participation standard. State the title and the core distinction.
2. **Core connection (5 minutes).** Teach the track's Core Connection: intention and impact are not the same thing; a person may intend one thing and create a different result; the work is being honest about both, not choosing one over the other.
3. **Scenario (5 minutes).** Read the track's Recognizable Scenario aloud (§5 table). Ask only: what did the person intend, what did they actually do, and how was it experienced?
4. **Seven-Step Application (20 minutes).** Work the track's steps in order — Recognize, Name, Explain, Separate, Interrupt, Practice, Carry It — using the scenario. Participants complete Workbook Lesson 4 §§4–8 alongside. The Practice step in every track keeps the goal and changes the delivery; teach that pattern explicitly, because it is the lesson's practical core.
5. **Accountability and Boundary Checks (5 minutes).** Teach both questions. In this lesson the boundary half is where false ownership is refused: participants are not responsible for another person's entire interpretation or feelings, and cannot guarantee being understood.
6. **Next Action and Carry-Forward (5 minutes).** Workbook §12 (listen for the information before defending the intention; one usable sentence is provided in the workbook) and the track's Carry-Forward line (§5 table), then the workbook's shared Carry-It Statement: “Keep the truth of what you meant. Take responsibility for what your behavior communicated.”

## 5. Track Distinctions — Lesson 4

| Track | Scenario (summary) | Interrupt question (participant-facing) | Practice response (participant-facing) | Carry-Forward (participant-facing) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Corrections | Staff member approaches; participant steps backward, raises their voice, and says “Back up,” intending to create space. Staff experiences the behavior as confrontational. Participant later says “I wasn't trying to threaten anybody” — which may be true and does not erase what the behavior communicated. | “What can I change without giving up what I was trying to accomplish?” | “I need some space. Please give me a second.” (The need remains; the delivery changes.) | “Mean what you mean. Watch what your behavior communicates.” |
| Reentry | Person stops answering calls because they do not want to argue with family. Family interprets the silence as disrespect or abandonment. Intention was to avoid conflict; impact is that communication breaks down. | “How can I accomplish the same goal without creating a new problem?” | “I don't want to argue right now. I'll call you tomorrow.” — then follow through. | “Good intentions still require responsible behavior.” |
| Veteran | Coworker makes a mistake; participant immediately gives blunt instructions to solve the problem quickly. Coworker feels embarrassed and becomes defensive. Participant thinks “I was just trying to help” — which may be true; the impact still matters. | “Can I keep the useful part of this response while changing the way I deliver it?” | “I noticed a problem. Want to work through it together?” | “A good purpose does not require a poor delivery.” |
| Workplace | Employee gives a short answer in a meeting to stay focused; coworker interprets it as rude. Employee says “I wasn't being rude.” Intention and impact are different questions. | “What did the other person actually hear?” | “I didn't mean it that way. Track-specific teaching points (facilitator notes):

- **Corrections:** The scenario involves staff perception and consequences. The lesson's own limit applies: “The participant does not have to agree with the interpretation to recognize the impact.” Do not turn the session into a ruling on any real incident, and do not promise that changed delivery will change any institutional outcome — the lesson makes no such claim.
- **Reentry:** The follow-through is part of the practice, not an extra: the practice line names a time (“I'll call you tomorrow”), and the lesson's Accountability Check includes repairing damage “when appropriate.” Teach the qualifier “when appropriate” as written.
- **Veteran:** The goal is translation, not abandonment: “The goal is not to abandon the strength. It is to understand its impact and translate it.” Do not teach directness itself as the problem.
- **Workplace:** The Name line for this track is “I did not intend to be dismissive, but I can see that my response sounded that way.” Note it holds both halves in one sentence; use it as the model for participants' own Name statements.

A discussion redirect is not a substitute lesson sequence.

The facilitator does not have to make the participant agree with the curriculum.

The draft invites discussion of this question, with passing allowed:

> **“Regardless of what happened around me, what is true about my behavior, what part is mine, and what can I do differently?”**

That is the facilitator-side expression of the WRH standard:

> **Understand the behavior.**
> **Tell the truth about the behavior.**
> **Own the behavior.**
> **Change what can be changed.**
