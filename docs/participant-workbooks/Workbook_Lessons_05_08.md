# CORE LESSON 5

# PERCEPTION VS. FACT

## 1. LESSON TITLE

### **Perception vs. Fact**

---

## 2. WHAT THIS LESSON IS ABOUT

What you perceive and what you know are not always the same thing.

Perception is the meaning you give to what you see, hear, or experience.

Fact is what can actually be established.

A perception can feel completely real and still be incomplete or wrong.

This lesson is not:

> “Ignore your instincts.”

It is:

> **Learn to check your interpretation against what you actually know.**

Where this shows up depends on your setting:

* **Corrections:** strong expectations about authority, staff motives, other participants, and what will happen next can shape how an ordinary interaction is interpreted.
* **Reentry:** a rejection, an unanswered phone call, a delayed response, or an unfamiliar instruction can be taken as proof that someone is against you.
* **Veteran:** reading a situation quickly can be useful, and it can also influence what you expect to see before you have enough information.
* **Workplace:** emails, facial expressions, feedback, meeting behavior, silence, tone, decisions, and changes in responsibilities are interpreted constantly, and incomplete information can become a complete story very quickly.

> **Your perception is information. It is not automatically proof.**

---

## 3. THE CORE DISTINCTION

> **Perception is the meaning I give to what happened. Fact is what can actually be established.**

# CORE LESSON 6

# CONTROL VS. INFLUENCE

## 1. LESSON TITLE

### **Control vs. Influence**

---

## 2. WHAT THIS LESSON IS ABOUT

There is a difference between what you control and what you can influence.

You do not control other people, institutional decisions, rules, schedules, or outcomes.

You do control your choices, behavior, preparation, communication, and response.

You can influence some outcomes through preparation, consistency, communication, and action — without controlling them.

There are three categories, and confusing them creates frustration:

* **Control** — what I can decide and do directly.
* **Influence** — what my actions can affect, without deciding the result.
* **Outside my control** — what belongs to other people, to an organization, or to circumstances.

Where this shows up depends on your setting:

* **Corrections:** rules may be fixed, schedules may be determined by others, and staff decisions may not go the way you want.
* **Reentry:** housing decisions, employment decisions, court requirements, family relationships, transportation, and finances all involve other people's choices.
* **Veteran:** civilian life may contain far less control over surrounding conditions than a setting with defined roles, procedures, and missions.
* **Workplace:** coworkers, management decisions, schedules, customer reactions, and company policies are not yours to control.

> **You don't control everything. That does not mean you control nothing.**

---

## 3. THE CORE DISTINCTION

> **What I control, what I can influence, and what is outside my control are three different categories. Knowing the difference prevents wasted effort and unnecessary conflict.**

# CORE LESSON 7

# ACCOUNTABILITY VS. BLAME

## 1. LESSON TITLE

### **Accountability vs. Blame**

---

## 2. WHAT THIS LESSON IS ABOUT

Accountability and blame are different.

Blame asks:

> “Whose fault is this?”

Accountability asks:

> **“What part belongs to me, and what do I do about it?”**

Accountability does not require pretending other people did nothing wrong. It requires being honest about your own part.

Accountability is also not the same as accepting all blame. A person can acknowledge their mistakes without claiming responsibility for everything that happened around them.

Circumstances influence behavior. They do not erase personal choice. Circumstances matter, and rebuilding still requires identifying the part that is actually yours.

Where this shows up depends on your setting:

* **Corrections:** conflict around responsibility can sound like “They made me do it” on one side and “He chose everything” on the other. Reality can be more complicated than either.
* **Reentry:** consequences connected to past decisions can be explained entirely through family, the system, or circumstances.
* **Veteran:** in a team, when something goes wrong, the first move can be to identify who failed — and to stop there.
* **Workplace:** when something goes wrong, the first move can be to prove it was not your fault, even when that is factually correct and a problem still needs solving.

> **Accountability is not taking all the blame. It is identifying your actual part.**

---

## 3. THE CORE DISTINCTION

# CORE LESSON 8

# OWNERSHIP VS. DEFENSE

## 1. LESSON TITLE

### **Ownership vs. Defense**

---

## 2. WHAT THIS LESSON IS ABOUT

When confronted with a problem, the first instinct may be to defend yourself.

That instinct can make sense.

But defending yourself and taking ownership are not the same thing.

You can correct something that is untrue **and** own what is true.

The three ideas in this lesson are different:

* **Explanation:** Why it happened.
* **Ownership:** What part was mine.
* **Defense:** Why I should not be held responsible.

An explanation can be true without being the whole truth. Explanation cannot replace ownership. Sometimes a defense is legitimate — a false accusation does not belong to you — and a defense should not erase accurate ownership of the part that is true.

Where this shows up depends on your setting:

* **Corrections:** being accused of something can feel high-stakes, and an inaccurate accusation does not automatically make every part of your own behavior acceptable.
* **Reentry:** questions about past behavior, employment history, relationships, missed obligations, or current responsibilities can invite an explanation for every problem.
* **Veteran:** a person used to being responsible for a mission, team, or outcome may explain the larger circumstances before examining their own decision.
* **Workplace:** feedback can prompt an immediate explanation of why a deadline was impossible, why another employee caused the delay, or why management created the problem.

> **We don't need to remove the explanation. We need to add ownership.**

---

## 3. THE CORE DISTINCTION

> **Explanation says why it happened. Ownership says what part was mine. Defense says why I should not be held responsible.**

## 9. PRACTICE

Choose the scenario for your setting. The four scenarios are different because the settings are different. You do not need to use a personal situation, and no names or personal history are required.

### Corrections Scenario

A participant is confronted about an argument and immediately responds, “He started it.” The other person's behavior may be relevant. The participant also threatened the person after the argument began. The defense explains the beginning of the conflict; it does not address the participant's own actions.

### Reentry Scenario

A person misses an appointment and explains, “Nobody reminded me.” That may be true. They also knew about the appointment and did not make transportation arrangements.

### Veteran Scenario

A project fails. The participant immediately explains that another person missed their task, resources were inadequate, leadership changed priorities, and circumstances changed. All of those things may be true. The participant also recognized a problem early and did not raise it.

### Workplace Scenario

A project misses its deadline. The employee says, “I couldn't finish because the information didn't come from the other employee.” The information was late. The employee also waited until the deadline approached before reporting the problem.

# BATCH 2 — PARTICIPANT WORKBOOK STANDARD

The four lessons use the same working sequence as Batch 1:

> **Recognize → Name → Explain → Separate → Interrupt → Practice → Accountability → Boundary → Next Action → Carry It**

The participant is never required to disclose trauma, identify another person, or tell a personal story to demonstrate mastery.

The exercises can be completed using:

* a current situation
* a past situation
* a fictional situation
* the provided scenario for your setting

The provided scenarios are setting-specific. The Corrections, Reentry, Veteran, and Workplace scenarios in each lesson come from that lesson's track in the core curriculum, and they are not interchangeable.

## The four lessons build a deliberate progression

**Lesson 5 — Perception vs. Fact**

> **Check what you think is happening against what you actually know.**

↓

**Lesson 6 — Control vs. Influence**

> **Put your effort where your effort can matter.**

↓

**Lesson 7 — Accountability vs. Blame**

> **Find your part without carrying theirs.**

↓

**Lesson 8 — Ownership vs. Defense**

> **Keep the explanation. Add accurate ownership.**

And the participant keeps returning to the same standard used in Batch 1:

> **Understand the behavior.**
> **Tell the truth about the behavior.**
> **Own the behavior.**
> **Change what can be changed.**
